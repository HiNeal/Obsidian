# A person you only met once recently and want to know more about

**Part2** — Describe a person you only met once recently and want to know more about. You should say: Who he/she is / When you met him/her / Why you want to know more about him/her / And explain how you feel about him/her

> I want to talk about a woman who runs a second-hand bookshop near my place. I only met her once, actually. It was about two months ago, and she's been on my mind ever since. It happened by accident, really. I'd gone in on a Saturday looking for nothing in particular, which is how I usually end up in that sort of shop. And I was there for nearly two hours. What struck me first was the shop itself. It's tiny, and the shelves go right up to the ceiling. There's no system to any of it, as far as I could tell. But she could put her hand on any title you mentioned in about thirty seconds. I said something about how she managed that, and that started the whole conversation. She told me she'd taught at a secondary school for twenty years. Then she walked out at forty-five and put her savings into the shop. Everybody told her it was commercial suicide, she said. The reason I want to know more about her is that she wasn't sentimental about it at all. I'd been expecting some speech about the smell of old books. Instead she talked about margins. About which customers actually buy rather than just browse. And about exactly why she'd refused to start selling coffee. She'd thought it through more carefully than I've thought through most of my own decisions, honestly. So what I feel is a kind of curiosity I haven't managed to satisfy. I've walked past a few times since and not gone in, which is a bit ridiculous. What I'd want to ask is whether she'd do it all again. And I genuinely can't predict which way she'd answer.

**Part3**

**1. What can we do to leave a good impression on people we meet for the first time?**
> Listening properly is the one thing that actually works. And it's rarer than you'd think. Most people spend a first conversation just waiting for their turn to talk. So anyone who asks a follow-up question stands out immediately. Beyond that, remembering small details. Where somebody works, something they mentioned in passing. Because it signals you were paying attention rather than performing. The surface things matter too, obviously. Turning up on time, looking like you've made an effort. But those only get you as far as neutral. What people actually remember is whether talking to you was enjoyable.

**2. What should we not talk about when we meet a person for the first time?**
> Money is the obvious one. Salaries, what somebody paid for their flat. Though that's culturally variable, and older relatives in China will ask you directly within minutes. Politics and religion are the standard advice. I'd add health problems, which people either don't want to discuss or will discuss at enormous length. The underlying principle is probably about consent, rather than the topic itself. Anything that forces the other person to either lie or reveal something they'd rather not is unfair that early on. Almost any subject becomes fine once they raise it themselves.

**3. How do people make friends in China?**
> Increasingly through shared activity, rather than proximity. My parents' generation made friends through their work unit or their neighbourhood. And those friendships lasted decades, because nobody moved. For people my age it's mostly university. And after that, interest groups. Running clubs, board game nights, that sort of thing, usually organised through group chats. What hasn't changed is the role of food. A friendship here isn't really established until you've eaten together several times. A surprising amount of the social work gets done around a table.

**4. On what occasions do people like to make friends?**
> Anywhere there's a shared task, mostly. People bond far more easily doing something side by side than facing each other trying to be interesting. Which is why work and university produce so many friendships. Travel is another one, because you're both slightly out of your depth. What I've noticed is that occasions explicitly designed for making friends tend to be the least effective. Networking events, that sort of thing. The pressure is too visible. It works much better when the friendship turns out to be a by-product of something else.

**5. Is it important to have the same hobbies and interests when making friends?**
> It helps enormously at the start. And matters much less later. Shared interests give you a reason to see somebody repeatedly, which is how most friendships get off the ground. You need that before you know each other well enough to just talk. But the friendships that last tend to survive people's interests changing completely. My closest friend and I have almost nothing in common now, in terms of what we actually do. What we share is a sense of humour and a fairly long history. And that turns out to be a lot more durable.

**6. What qualities make true friends?**
> Reliability, more than anything. Though it's the least glamorous answer available. Somebody who does what they said they'd do, and turns up when it's inconvenient for them. Beyond that, the ability to disagree with you without it becoming a crisis. A friend who only ever agrees isn't much use to anyone. And discretion, which people badly underrate. Knowing that something you said in a bad moment won't be repeated is exactly what allows you to be honest. Without it, a friendship tends to stay fairly shallow.

# A person who is good at making things by hand

**Part2** — Describe a person who is good at making things by hand. You should say: Who this person is / What he/she makes / What materials or technologies he/she uses / And why you think this person is good at making things by hand

> The person I'd describe is a former colleague of mine, an engineer called Zhou. He builds mechanical keyboards by hand. We sat opposite each other for about three years, which is how I ended up knowing far more about this than I ever intended to. What he makes are custom keyboards, which sounds trivial until you see one. The case is machined aluminium, and he designs the inside layout himself. That includes a layer of foam that changes how the whole thing sounds. Then he lubricates each switch individually, with a brush. There are about ninety of them, so that's a whole evening gone. The materials are a bit of a mixture. Aluminium and brass for the weight, various plastics for the switches, and more and more wood for the cases lately. He's started machining those himself, on a small CNC mill in his flat. He also solders every joint by hand rather than buying the ready-made boards, which he thinks of as cheating. Why is he good at it? Patience, I'd say, rather than any obvious craft talent. He'll take a whole board apart because one key sounds slightly different from the others. Which, I should say, is a difference I genuinely cannot hear. I watched him do that once and found it almost unbearable. The other thing is that he isn't precious about failure. A ruined case just gets thrown out and started again, without much comment. That's the part I admire, and the part I can't copy, because I'd have talked myself into accepting it.

**Part3**

**1. Do you think art classes are important for children?**
> Important, though perhaps not for the reasons schools usually give. The argument you normally hear is about creativity, which is a bit vague. What I'd defend is more concrete. Art classes are one of the few places where a child works on something for weeks with no correct answer waiting at the end. Almost everything else in school is marked against a standard. There's also the simple matter of finding out what you're drawn to. And you can't discover you love making things if nobody ever puts the materials in front of you.

**2. Why do some children enjoy making things by hand?**
> Partly because the results are visible, which most schoolwork isn't. You finish something and it exists. You can hold it. And nobody has to mark it for you to know whether it worked. There's also something about the physicality. Children spend an enormous amount of time being told to sit still and think. And making things gives their hands something to do. And I suspect there's a control element too. A child has very little authority over anything in their life. But they have complete authority over a lump of clay.

**3. Do children these days still like doing arts and crafts?**
> Fewer than before, I'd say. Though I'm wary of sounding nostalgic about it. The competition for their attention is simply fiercer. A screen offers a faster reward than a craft that takes three weekends. That said, there's been a genuine revival online. Children learn techniques from videos and then show the results to an audience, which my generation never had. So it may be less that interest has declined. And more that it's moved somewhere their parents can't see it.

**4. What impact can artistic buildings have on society?**
> A considerable one. And mostly at the level of how people feel about where they live, rather than anything measurable. A well-designed public building gives a city somewhere people are actually willing to meet. You can see the difference in whether a square gets used or just walked across. There's a signalling effect too. Spending money on a beautiful library says something about what a place values. The risk, obviously, is buildings designed to impress visitors rather than serve residents. Which is a failure however striking the photographs are.

**5. Is it good to have creative people in a team?**
> Very much so. Though a team of nothing but creative people would be a nightmare. What creative people contribute is a willingness to question the brief. To ask whether the problem has been framed correctly in the first place. Which is where most bad work originates. But that only turns into anything if somebody alongside them will actually finish it. The strongest teams I've been on had one person generating far too many ideas. And another quietly making one of them real.

**6. Does art, like music or theater, have an impact on society?**
> It does, though the effect is slow and hard to point at. Art gives a society a way of discussing things it can't yet discuss directly. Which is why controversial plays and songs tend to appear slightly before the public conversation catches up. There's a more practical side too. A city with a live music scene or decent theatres holds on to its young people. What I'd resist is the claim that art improves people morally. I don't think the evidence supports that. And it isn't a burden art needs to carry.

# A person you know who did something difficult and was successful

**Part2** — Describe a person you know who did something difficult and was successful. You should say: Who this person is / What difficult thing this person did / Why this person was successful / And explain how you feel about this person

> The person I want to describe is a friend from university called Ming. He spent about four years building a video channel about Shenzhen, and now he makes his living from it. What made it difficult is that he started with nothing that would obviously work. He'd graduated with a fairly ordinary degree and taken a job he disliked. Then he started filming the city at weekends. Specifically the urban villages and the old buildings, so the parts of Shenzhen that are disappearing fastest. His first year produced almost no audience at all. I remember watching one of his videos that had two hundred views. And I told him, in what I thought was a kind way, that he'd made his point. The turn came from getting more specific, not more popular. He stopped making videos about Shenzhen in general. Instead he started documenting single buildings before demolition, going back again and again, talking to the people living there. Baishizhou was the one that changed things. It's the largest urban village clearance the city has seen, and he'd been filming there for two years before it became news. Why did he succeed? I'd say it's that he was willing to narrow the whole thing down when the broad version wasn't working. Everyone tells you to persist. Almost nobody tells you to get smaller. How I feel about him is straightforwardly admiring, and a bit uncomfortable as well, because I took the secure job and he didn't.

**Part3**

**1. Other than making money, what is more important for a company?**
> Whether it's still there in ten years, essentially. Profit is a measure of how you're doing, rather than what you're for. And companies that treat it as the whole objective tend to make decisions that look brilliant for three years and hollow out everything afterwards. Beyond durability, I'd point to how it treats the people who work there. Partly for the obvious reason, and partly because staff turnover is expensive and slow to show up in the accounts. And reputation, which is the one asset you can't buy back quickly once you've spent it.

**2. Is it easy to start up a company?**
> Easy to start, difficult to survive. Registering a company here takes days and costs very little. Shenzhen in particular has made that about as frictionless as it gets. What's hard is everything afterwards. You're competing with people who have more capital and can absorb losses you can't. Ming's experience is fairly typical. The idea was the easy part. The two years with no income were the real test. Most failures aren't bad ideas. They're good ideas that ran out of money.

**3. In which sectors are companies more likely to succeed in China?**
> Anything built on top of the delivery and payments infrastructure. Because that's already in place, and it's genuinely world-class. Food and beverage keeps producing successes, despite being fiercely competitive. Beyond that, the obvious answer is technology. Particularly anything around electric vehicles and renewables, where the state has been pushing hard and the supply chain is now enormous. What's become much harder is anything that competes directly with the big platforms. They can simply outspend you until you disappear.

**4. Is it difficult to do business in today’s world?**
> Harder in some ways and far easier in others. What's harder is that competition is global by default now. So margins have been squeezed everywhere. Regulation is also considerably more complex than it was. But the things that used to be genuinely difficult have collapsed in cost. Reaching customers. Taking payments. Finding suppliers. Ming could never have built an audience without the platforms. And fifteen years ago that would have needed money he didn't have.

**5. How can companies help protect the environment?**
> The honest answer is that most of the meaningful decisions are unglamorous. Changing what you source and how you ship it matters far more than anything visible to customers. Logistics and packaging are where most of the footprint sits. There's a strong case for transparency too. Publishing the numbers even when they're unflattering, because it makes the commitments checkable. What I'm sceptical about is the marketing version. Where a company runs a campaign about recycling while its actual operations are unchanged. That's a reputational strategy, not an environmental one.

**6. Does money equal success?**
> Clearly not. Though I'd resist the opposite cliché as well. Money is one measure of success, and a fairly reliable one. Something making none of it has failed by most definitions. Where it breaks down is that it measures scale, rather than whether the thing was worth doing. Ming earns considerably less than I do. And nobody who knows us both would describe him as less successful. What he got instead was the work being his.

# A person you know who would like to choose a career in the medical field (e.g. a doctor, a nurse)

**Part2** — Describe a person you know who would like to choose a career in the medical field (e.g. a doctor, a nurse). You should say: When you knew him/her / When he/she started to think about that / What he/she would like to do / And explain why he/she would like to choose this career

> I'd like to talk about my cousin Lin. She's two years older than me, and she's a paediatric nurse here in Shenzhen now. I've known her my whole life, obviously, since our mothers are sisters and we spent every holiday together growing up. But I'd say I only really got to know her as an adult in the last few years, once we could have conversations that weren't about who'd taken whose toy. As for when she started thinking about it, she'll tell you it was our grandfather's illness. Which is probably half true. He was in hospital for a long stretch when she was about fifteen, and she spent most of that summer on the ward. What she talks about isn't the doctors, interestingly. It's one particular nurse, who explained what was happening in language she could actually follow, when everyone else was talking over her head. What she does now is work with children, and she's fairly clear-eyed about it. She'd done placements before she qualified, and she describes the job as exhausting and sometimes grim. She's not romantic about it in the slightest. Why does she want it? I think it's that she's unusually good at staying calm around distress. I'm not, and I notice it in her. Most of the family assumed she'd chosen medicine for the security. I'm fairly sure they've got that backwards, actually. She'd have had an easier life doing almost anything else.

**Part3**

**1. Do you think being a doctor is easy or difficult?**
> Difficult. And difficult in ways that aren't obvious from outside. The training is long, and the competition to get in is severe. But I'd say the harder part is what the job asks of you emotionally. You're making consequential decisions while tired. And delivering bad news to people who'll remember that conversation for the rest of their lives. There's also the accumulation of it. A doctor sees more suffering in a year than most of us see in a decade. What looks like professional detachment from outside is usually something that had to be learnt, at some cost.

**2. Do you think learning biology is interesting for children?**
> For many of them, yes. Because it's the one science that's obviously about them. You can show a child a leaf, or a beetle, or their own pulse. And none of that requires the abstraction that physics does. Where it tends to lose them is the point where it becomes vocabulary. A subject that was about living things turns into a list of terms to memorise for a test. My own interest survived roughly until we started being examined on the parts of a cell.

**3. Why do some children want to become doctors?**
> The straightforward reason is that it's one of the few jobs children can actually picture. They've met a doctor. They've seen what one does. Most professions are completely invisible to a child. Beyond that, there's the status attached to it, which parents transmit fairly unsubtly. And I think there's a genuine element too. Children are quite drawn to the idea of being useful in an emergency. Whether that survives contact with a biochemistry exam is another question.

**4. Do you think governments should put a large amount of money into medical research?**
> Yes. And I'd argue it's among the better uses of public money, with one caveat. The case for it is that basic research is exactly the kind of thing markets underfund. The payoff is decades away and can't be captured by whoever paid for it. Left to companies, you get research into profitable conditions rather than important ones. The caveat is that money alone doesn't produce results. Funding tied to short-term publication targets tends to generate a lot of activity and not much discovery.

**5. Why is some doctors’ pay high and others’ low?**
> Partly specialisation, partly economics. Surgeons train for far longer and carry more risk, so the pay reflects both the scarcity and the liability. Beyond that, a lot of it comes down to where you work rather than what you do. A doctor in a major city hospital and one in a rural clinic may do similar work for very different money. In China there's an extra factor. The public system sets the salary. So a great deal of the variation is about the institution, not the individual.

**6. Do you think doctors should be paid more? Why?**
> Generally yes. Particularly at the lower end, and particularly for nurses. My cousin's starting salary was modest, for a job involving twelve-hour shifts and genuine responsibility. And the countries that underpay nursing staff invariably end up with shortages. The stronger argument isn't really about fairness, though. It's about who the profession attracts and retains. If the pay is poor relative to the training required, capable people quietly go elsewhere. And you notice the consequences about fifteen years later, when it's expensive to fix.

# A successful sportsperson you admire

**Part2** — Describe a successful sportsperson you admire. You should say: Who he/she is / What you know about him/her / What he/she is like in real life / What achievement he/she has made / And explain why you admire him/her

> The person I'd choose is Su Bingtian, the sprinter. Most people here know him from the Tokyo Olympics. He ran nine eight three in the hundred metres semi-final, which made him the first Chinese man ever to reach an Olympic final in that event. He's also from Guangdong, so people around here take a particular interest. What I knew about him before that was fairly limited. He'd been around for years, winning things regionally, and was generally described as having reached his ceiling. He was thirty-two at the time, which in sprinting is basically retirement age. And he'd already been written off more than once after injuries. What I find compelling is what he did in his late twenties, when by any normal reading his career was winding down. He changed his starting leg. Which sounds like a small thing and is apparently close to relearning the event from scratch. His times actually got worse at first. He stuck with it for two seasons. As for what he's like in real life, what comes across in interviews is how analytical he is. He talks about his own running the way an engineer talks about a machine, breaking it into parts. He did a postgraduate degree, and he coaches at a university now. There's none of the theatrical self-belief you often get from sprinters. Why I admire him is the willingness to get worse on purpose in order to get better later. Most of us protect whatever is almost working. He took apart the thing he was already good at, at an age when nobody would have blamed him for leaving it alone.

**Part3**

**1. Should students have physical education and do sports at school?**
> Certainly. Though I'd want it taught better than it was for me. The health argument is well established and barely worth restating. What I'd emphasise instead is that sport teaches losing, which very little else in school does. You can be beaten on a Tuesday and play again on a Thursday. And that repetition does something useful to your relationship with failure. My reservation is about how it's often delivered. A class that consists of picking teams and humiliating whoever goes last teaches the opposite lesson quite efficiently.

**2. What qualities should an athlete have?**
> Physically, whatever their event demands, which is fairly obvious. The interesting qualities are the others. The capacity to train without an audience, for one. Almost all of the work happens in an empty facility on an ordinary morning. Then the ability to treat losing as information, rather than as a verdict on yourself. And I'd add a certain willingness to be coached. Which sounds passive and isn't. It means accepting that somebody watching you knows something about your technique that you can't feel from the inside.

**3. Is talent important in sports?**
> It's the entry ticket, rather than the whole thing. At the top level everyone is talented. So talent stops distinguishing anyone, and the differences come from training, coaching and luck with injuries. Where talent genuinely matters is early. It determines whether a child gets selected into the system that provides the coaching. And that's largely a matter of physiology they had no say in. So I'd put it this way. Talent decides who gets the opportunity. Almost everything after that is work.

**4. Is it easy to identify children’s talents?**
> Not particularly. And the attempts to do it systematically have a poor record. Children develop at wildly different rates. So what looks like ability at nine is frequently just being born in January and being bigger than everyone else. There's good evidence of that bias across several sports. What's more reliable is noticing what a child returns to voluntarily. Because that says something about temperament rather than aptitude. The risk of early identification is that you invest heavily in one child and stop watching the others.

**5. What is the most popular sport in your country?**
> Basketball, by a distance, in terms of both participation and audience. Football has the larger following abroad, but a complicated relationship with the domestic game here. Beyond those, badminton and table tennis are played everywhere, and produce most of our Olympic success. And running has grown enormously in the last decade. City marathons are now oversubscribed by a wide margin. What's shifted is that exercise has become a lifestyle marker for urban professionals, rather than something you did at school.

**6. Why are there so few top athletes?**
> Because the filter is brutal at every stage. And most of it isn't about ability. You need the physiology, which is rare enough. But you also need to have been spotted. To have had access to coaching. To have avoided one bad injury at the wrong moment. And to have had a family who could absorb the cost and the risk. Any one of those failing removes you. What's striking about someone like Su Bingtian is how many of those had to break in his favour before the training even became relevant.

# Someone who is older than you that you admire

**Part2** — Describe someone who is older than you that you admire. You should say: Who this person is / How you knew this person / What kinds of things you like to do together / and explain how you feel about this person

> The person I'd like to talk about is my grandmother. She's in her late seventies, and she still lives in the urban village where my mother grew up, in the north of Shenzhen. I've known her my whole life, obviously. But our relationship changed quite a lot when I was around sixteen and started going over on my own, rather than being taken. That's when she stopped treating me as a child, and I noticed it immediately. What we do together is pretty unremarkable, and that's rather the point. She's turned the flat roof of the old house into a vegetable garden. I carry soil up the stairs for her, because she won't ask anyone else. We cook, and she gives instructions rather than measurements, which I've stopped finding annoying. And we walk, slowly, around streets where she seems to know a remarkable number of people. Why I admire her is harder to put simply. She was born when Shenzhen was farmland and fishing villages. She left school at twelve. And she's watched one of the largest cities on earth assemble itself around her house. She's completely without self-pity about any of it. And equally without nostalgia, which is the part that surprises people. She doesn't think the past was better. She just doesn't think the present owes her anything either. There's also a lack of anxiety about her that I find almost enviable, and which I've completely failed to inherit.

**Part3**

**1. What can young and old people learn from each other?**
> In both directions, though what's transferred is different in kind. From older people you get perspective, essentially, the knowledge that a great many things that feel enormous now will have dissolved in five years. They've watched several crises arrive and pass. What travels the other way is more practical, and technology is the obvious example. Though I'd also say younger people often bring a different set of assumptions about how life ought to be lived, which can be quietly liberating. The difficulty is that both sides tend to assume the other has nothing to teach them.

**2. Has old people's life quality improved when compared to the past?**
> Enormously, on almost every measurable dimension. Life expectancy in China has risen dramatically, healthcare is far more accessible than in my grandmother's youth. And the material conditions aren't comparable. Where I'd hesitate is on the social side. The extended family used to guarantee that an older person lived surrounded by people. And urbanisation has largely dismantled that, so you now have a generation that's materially comfortable and considerably more isolated. Loneliness is the problem that's got worse while everything else improved.

**3. Why is there a generation gap between the young and the old?**
> Because the world they formed their assumptions in no longer exists, and China is an extreme case of that. My grandmother's childhood and mine happened in what are effectively different countries. So the gap isn't really about age, it's about the rate of change. Where there's stability across generations, the gap is much smaller. Beyond that, there's the ordinary business of young people needing to differentiate themselves, which every generation does. That part is permanent, whereas the sheer scale of it here is historically specific.

**4. What do old people usually do in their daily life?**
> It varies enormously by health and by city, but there's a fairly recognisable pattern. Mornings are the active part of the day. So parks fill with people exercising, dancing and playing chess well before eight. A lot of them are looking after grandchildren. Which is effectively a full-time job and one the whole economy quietly depends on. Beyond that, shopping daily rather than weekly, which is partly habit and partly a social outing. What's changed recently is how much of their time is now spent on smartphones, which surprised everyone.

**5. What sports are popular among older people?**
> Anything low-impact and social, essentially. Tai chi is the obvious one. And square dancing is genuinely enormous here, to the point where it generates disputes about noise in most residential areas. Walking, badminton, and swimming where there's a pool. What these have in common is that they're done in company and require no equipment. Which I suspect matters more than the physical benefit. My grandmother would say she goes for the conversation and treats the exercise as incidental.

**6. What kinds of things do the elderly remember for a long time?**
> The emotionally charged ones, almost exclusively, and they're rarely the events you'd expect. My grandmother can describe a particular meal from sixty years ago in complete detail and can't reliably remember what she did last Tuesday. Which I gather is quite normal. What seems to survive is anything tied to a strong feeling, so births, losses, and moments of fear or relief. There's also a documented tendency for memories from adolescence and early adulthood to be the most vivid. Which explains why that period comes up so often in their stories.

# A person you know who really likes taking photos

**Part2** — Describe a person you know who really likes taking photos. You should say: Who the person is / When and how you got to know him/her / Where he/she takes photos / And explain how you feel about him/her

> The person I'd describe is Ming, a friend from university. He's photographed and filmed Shenzhen pretty obsessively for about six years, and he does it for a living now. We met in our first year, in Huizhou, where we both studied. Then we both came back to Shenzhen afterwards and stayed in touch. It started as a weekend thing, while he was doing a job he didn't like. He'd go out on Saturdays with a camera and shoot the urban villages. That's the dense, low-rise housing most of this city was built on top of, and it's being cleared district by district. So what was meant to be a distraction turned into the thing he does. Where he shoots is what makes him unusual. He has no interest in the skyline at all, which is what every visitor photographs. He'll tell you the glass towers are the least interesting thing here. Instead it's interiors, staircases, shopfronts. The handshake buildings, where you can literally reach out of your window and touch the block opposite. And he goes back to the same places over and over, for months. That's how he ended up with a full record of Baishizhou before it came down. How I feel about him is a mix of admiration and mild exasperation, honestly. He's late to everything, because he's stopped to photograph a doorway. But he's made something that will genuinely matter later. And he did it without ever announcing that he was doing it.

**Part3**

**1. Why are some people keen on taking photos?**
> Several quite different reasons, I think, and they get lumped together unhelpfully. For some it's memory, straightforwardly, a way of holding on to something they know they'll forget. For others it's about noticing, since carrying a camera changes how you look at an ordinary street. And my friend would say the photograph is almost a by-product of that attention. And then there's the social motivation, photographing things in order to show them, which has become dominant with phones. That last one gets criticised heavily, though I'm not convinced wanting to share what you've seen is a modern failing.

**2. What kind of photos do people often take?**
> Overwhelmingly people, and overwhelmingly people they know. Beyond that, food, which in China is practically a genre of its own. And whatever they happen to be standing in front of while travelling. What's changed is the volume rather than the subject matter, since the marginal cost of a photograph is now zero and people take forty where they'd once have taken one. The unintended effect is that far fewer get looked at afterwards. Because nobody edits, so they accumulate somewhere and are never revisited.

**3. Do you think it’s ok to take photos everywhere?**
> No, and the boundaries are considerably less obvious than people assume. Hospitals, other people's children, anywhere somebody hasn't consented to being photographed. And quite a lot of religious sites where it's actively disrespectful. The complication is that a rule that made sense when photographs were rare doesn't transfer easily. Because now everything is potentially permanent and searchable. What I'd apply is a fairly simple test: whether the person in the frame would mind if they knew. That covers most cases without needing a rulebook.

**4. What are the downsides of constantly taking photos while traveling?**
> The main one is that you stop experiencing the thing itself. There's research suggesting that photographing something actually weakens your memory of it, which is precisely backwards from the intention. Beyond that, it distorts how you travel, because you start planning around the photographs rather than around what you'd actually like to see. And you end up queuing at a viewpoint you'd have walked past otherwise. And it affects the places themselves, since somewhere that becomes photogenic online gets overwhelmed within a season.

**5. Some people don't mind others walking into their shot when they're taking photos. What do you think?**
> I'd say they're being sensible, on the whole. Insisting on an empty frame in a public place is a slightly unreasonable demand, given that other people have as much right to be there as you do. There's also an argument that the presence of other people is often what makes the photograph honest, since an artificially emptied street is a fiction. Where I'd have sympathy is if somebody is deliberately obstructing you, or if it's a genuinely once-off occasion like a wedding, where the stakes are rather different.

**6. When do people like taking photos?**

> Uncertainty, principally, rather than workload. People cope remarkably well with being extremely busy if they know what's expected and believe it will end. # A person who taught you a new skill

**Part2** — Describe a person who taught you a new skill. You should say: Who this person was / What the skill was / How you learned it / And explain how you felt about this person

**1. Why should children learn new skills?**
> The straightforward answer is that skills compound, and the ones learnt young are learnt faster and more durably. But I'd put the emphasis elsewhere. What a child gets from learning a skill is evidence that they can move from incompetent to competent. And having that evidence changes what they're willing to attempt later. A child who has learnt one difficult thing approaches the next one differently. The specific skill matters considerably less than that. Which is why I'm relaxed about whether it's piano or swimming or coding.

**2. Where can children learn new skills?**
> Far more places than a generation ago, which is the significant change. School is the obvious one and still the most structured. But an enormous amount now happens online. And children teach themselves things through video that they'd previously have needed a tutor for. Beyond that, from each other, which is consistently underrated, since children explain things to one another in language that actually lands. And from family, particularly the practical skills that don't survive being formalised, cooking and repairing things and so on.

> By demonstrating rather than explaining, mostly, and then by getting out of the way sooner than feels comfortable. **1. Do you think children should begin learning history from an early age?**
> Yes, though not in the form it usually takes. What's typically taught early is a sequence of names and dates. Which is the least valuable part and the part most likely to put a child off permanently. What children can grasp remarkably young is that people in the past lived differently and had reasons for what they did. And that's the actual historical skill. So I'd start early and start with how people lived rather than with what happened. The chronology can arrive later, once there's something for it to attach to.

**2. At what age do you think people should begin to learn history?**
> Informally, from about four or five, which is when children start asking what things were like when you were small. That's a historical question. Formal history, in the sense of a subject with periods and causes, probably works from eight or nine, once a child can hold a sense of time longer than their own life. What I'd avoid is the current tendency to postpone anything complicated, since children handle moral ambiguity considerably better than curricula assume.

**3. How do children learn history before they attend school?**
> Almost entirely through family stories, which is where most historical consciousness originates. A grandparent describing a childhood without electricity does more than any textbook. **4. Is it important to learn how people in the past lived their lives?**
> Very, and I'd argue it's more important than knowing the events. Understanding how people lived is what makes the past comprehensible rather than merely strange. **6. Why do people want to be popular?**
> Partly something fairly deep, in that humans are a social species and exclusion was historically dangerous. So the desire for acceptance isn't a character flaw. Beyond that, popularity is instrumental, since it opens doors and provides opportunities, and people are not wrong to want it. What's changed is that it's become measurable. And a visible number attached to how liked you are creates a pressure that previous generations simply didn't face. I'd distinguish wanting to be liked from wanting to be counted. And it's the second one that seems to do damage.

# A person you know who has a successful business

**Part2** — Describe a person you know who has a successful business. You should say: Who this person is / How you got to know him/her / Why and how he/she started the business / What business he/she does / And explain why you think the business is successful

> The person I'd describe is Ming, a friend from university. He's built a video channel about Shenzhen into something that supports him and now employs two other people. I've known him about twelve years, since we both studied in Huizhou. Though I'd say I only understood what he does once the numbers got big enough for it to be a business rather than a hobby. Why he started it is straightforward. He'd taken an ordinary job after graduating and disliked it fairly comprehensively. So the filming began at weekends, as something that was his. How he started it was much less straightforward. There was no audience for it, and no obvious way it would ever pay. What he makes are short documentaries about the parts of Shenzhen that are disappearing. The urban villages, the factory buildings, the markets. And he goes back to a place over and over for months, rather than filming it once. That's the actual product, I think. Not the footage, but the access, because by the time he films, the residents know him. The money comes from a mixture of platform payments, commissioned work for museums and developers, and a book that did unexpectedly well. Why I'd call it successful isn't really the income, which is decent rather than remarkable. It's that he found a subject nobody else was covering seriously. And he got there by narrowing his idea down when the broad version failed.

**Part3**

**1. Why do some people start their own business?**
> Autonomy, most commonly, which in Ming's case was the entire motivation. A lot of people start things not because they've spotted an opportunity but because they can't tolerate being managed. Beyond that, there's the financial case, since employment has a ceiling and ownership doesn't. And a third group who've identified something specific that isn't being provided and find the gap genuinely irritating. What's rarer than the mythology suggests is people motivated by risk-taking as such.

**2. Should governments provide financial support to start-ups?**
> Some support, yes, though I'd be careful about the form. The case for it is that new businesses face a financing problem banks handle badly, since there's no track record to lend against. What works less well is direct subsidy, which tends to attract businesses built around obtaining the subsidy. Tax treatment, simplified registration and access to credit seem more effective. Shenzhen does the administrative side about as well as anywhere in China.

**3. Do most people prefer shopping at big stores or small stores?**
> It depends entirely on what they're buying, and the pattern has become quite stable. For anything standardised, groceries, electronics, people go where it's cheapest and most convenient, which means large stores or online. For anything where they want specificity or advice, small shops retain an advantage. And there's been a modest revival of that in the cities. What's genuinely changed is that the middle has collapsed. Medium-sized shops that were neither cheap nor distinctive have largely disappeared, and that's where most of the closures have been.

**4. What makes a business successful?**
> Solving a problem people will pay to have solved, first of all, which eliminates most failed businesses. Beyond that, cash management, which is unglamorous and kills more companies than bad ideas do. Then the ability to change your mind about your own plan when the evidence contradicts it. Which is what Ming did when he narrowed his subject. And timing, which people convert into a story about foresight afterwards but is largely luck.

**5. What makes a business fail?**
> Running out of money before finding the customers, in the overwhelming majority of cases. Underneath that there's usually one of two errors: building something nobody particularly wanted, or being right about the demand and wrong about the economics, so every sale loses money. Beyond those, growing too quickly, which strains cash and quality simultaneously. And founders who can't delegate, which caps the business at whatever one person can personally oversee. Very few failures are dramatic. Most are a slow accumulation of months that didn't quite work.

**6. Is it easy to set up a new business in your country?**
> Administratively, extremely easy now, and that's a real change. Registration takes days rather than months, and the digital infrastructure for payments and delivery is as good as anywhere in the world. So a small operator can reach customers without much capital. What's difficult is the competition, since low barriers mean everyone else can enter too. And margins in most consumer sectors are thin. So the honest answer is that setting one up is easy and keeping it alive for three years is not.

# A person who loves to grow plants (e.g. vegetables, flowers) at home or in the garden

**Part2** — Describe a person who loves to grow plants (e.g. vegetables, flowers) at home or in the garden. You should say: Who this person is / What plants he/she grows / How he/she grows the plants / And explain why he/she loves growing plants

> The person I'd like to describe is my grandmother. She's in her late seventies, and she's turned the flat roof of her house into something close to a smallholding. She lives in one of the urban villages in the north of Shenzhen, in a house my grandfather built himself. What she grows is almost entirely edible, which is typical of her generation. There's spring onion in several containers, which she'd say is non-negotiable. Chillies. Garlic chives. Some tomatoes that do reasonably well in spring, before the heat kills them. And a lot of leafy greens whose English names I've never known. There's exactly one flowering plant, an orchid somebody gave her, and she tolerates it rather than tends it. How she grows them is where it gets impressive. She mixes her own soil. She composts vegetable scraps in a bucket that my grandfather objected to for about twenty years. And she saves seed from year to year instead of buying it. She waters at specific times, early morning and after dark, and she has strong views about the neighbours who water at midday. None of this comes from books. It comes from having grown up in a farming village, right here, before any of this was a city. Why she loves it is something I've thought about more than she has. Part of it is practical. She genuinely believes shop vegetables taste of nothing, and she may well be right. But I think the bigger part is that it gives her something that responds to attention. She's been retired for twenty-five years. The roof is the one thing that would notice if she stopped.

**Part3**

**1. What are the advantages of growing vegetables or flowers at home?**
> Freshness and taste, first of all. Which is the reason most people would give and which is genuinely true for things like herbs and tomatoes that deteriorate quickly. Beyond that, there's the food-safety dimension. Which matters more here than in some countries and is a real motivation for older people. But the advantages that seem to matter most are the ones nobody lists. It's daily physical activity, it gives structure to a retired person's day. And it produces something to give away, which is its own small social economy.

**2. Do many people grow vegetables or flowers at home in your country?**
> A great many, and it's one of the more visible features of Chinese urban life. Older people in particular will cultivate any available surface, balconies, stairwells, the strip of soil beside a compound wall. And occasionally patches of public land in a way that causes mild municipal friction. There's a generational element, since most of them grew up rural and the knowledge came with them. Among younger people it's grown recently, though that's more about houseplants and aesthetics than about food.

**3. Is it easy to grow plants at home?**
> Easy to start and surprisingly easy to fail at, which is why so many people try once and give up. The hard constraints in a flat are light and consistency, and light is the one people misjudge, since a room that seems bright to a human is fairly dim to a tomato. The other difficulty is that plants need attention on their schedule rather than yours. And a week away in summer can undo months. What makes my grandmother good at it is simply that she's never away.

**4. Why do some people like to grow plants?**
> Because it operates on a timescale almost nothing else in modern life does. You do something in March and find out in July. And that's a rhythm people find restorative precisely because it can't be accelerated. There's also the element of care, in that a plant is something dependent on you that doesn't ask for conversation. Which suits some temperaments very well. And the straightforward satisfaction of having made something edible, which is rare for anyone whose work is abstract.

**5. Why do some people prefer to grow their own fruits and vegetables instead of buying them from the market?**
> Taste and control, mostly. Anyone who has eaten a tomato off their own balcony finds the supermarket version genuinely disappointing. And that's not sentimentality, it's about how commercial varieties are bred for transport. The control dimension is about knowing what's gone into it. Which for older Chinese people connects to some fairly well-founded historical anxieties about food safety. And there's a cost argument for herbs specifically, since a packet of coriander costs more than a season's worth of growing it.

**6. Do you think students should learn to grow plants?**
> Yes, and I'd make it fairly practical rather than theoretical. Growing something teaches patience and consequence in a way that's difficult to teach directly, since the plant simply dies if neglected and no amount of explanation substitutes for that. It also connects food to its origin, which is genuinely obscure to children raised in cities. What I'd avoid is making it another assessed subject. The value is in the doing, and I'm not sure it survives being examined.

# A person you know who is very organized

**Part2** — Describe a person you know who is very organized. You should say: Who this person is / What this person usually does to stay organized / In what situations you have noticed this quality / And explain why you think being organized is important to him/her

> The person I'd describe is Yaojin, my team lead. He's organised to a degree I found slightly unnerving at first, and now rely on completely. What he does to stay organised is less elaborate than you'd expect, which I think is the point. He keeps one system rather than several, and that's where most people go wrong. Everything goes into a single list. And it gets processed at fixed times, twice a day, rather than reacting as things come in. He writes down what he's agreed to in front of you, which has the side effect of making everyone else more careful about what they ask him for. And he plans backwards from deadlines. So a project due in six weeks has intermediate dates that exist from day one, instead of being invented in week five. Where I've noticed it most is when something goes wrong. We had a release date pulled forward by ten days last year. For most teams that would have been a crisis. He'd built slack into the schedule specifically because that happens, and the whole thing got absorbed without anyone working a weekend. That's when I understood that his organisation isn't about tidiness at all. Why does it matter to him? I think it buys him calm. He's said something to that effect, that the system exists so he doesn't have to hold anything in his head. What it actually produces is capacity. And that's the part I've tried to copy.

**Part3**

**1. Why is it important for people to be organized?**
> The obvious answer is efficiency, though I'd argue that's the smaller benefit. What being organised actually buys is mental capacity, since anything you're trying to remember is occupying attention you could be using for the work itself. Chen's version of this is that her system exists so that she doesn't have to think about what she's forgetting. The second benefit is reliability in the eyes of other people, which compounds over a career. Because the person who consistently does what they said gets given the interesting work.

**2. Do you think children should learn to be organized from a young age?**
> Yes, though with a fairly important caveat about method. What children can absorb early is the habit of putting things back and of breaking a task into steps. And those transfer to almost everything later. What doesn't work is imposing an adult system on a child, since a planner that a parent designed and enforces produces compliance rather than organisation. The test is whether the child maintains it when nobody is checking. I'd start with very small domains, one shelf, one bag, and let the ownership be real.

**3. Are organized people more likely to be successful at work or in their studies?**
> On balance yes, though the relationship is weaker than the productivity literature suggests. Organisation is a multiplier rather than a cause. So it amplifies whatever ability is already there and does nothing on its own. There are obviously disorganised people who are exceptional, usually in fields where a single insight outweighs consistent delivery. But in most work, the advantage compounds quietly: the organised person misses fewer deadlines, forgets fewer commitments. And accumulates a reputation that opens doors over about a decade.

**4. What can people do to become more organized?**
> Reduce the number of systems, first, since most disorganised people I know have four half-maintained ones. Then externalise everything, because the attempt to remember is itself the problem. Beyond that, process at fixed times rather than continuously. Which is the habit I copied from Chen and which made the most difference. And build in more slack than seems necessary, since the plans that collapse are the ones with no tolerance for the entirely predictable fact that something will go wrong.

**5. Should parents teach their children to be organized?**
> They should, though by demonstration rather than instruction, and with more patience than most manage. The common failure is that parents organise things for the child, which is faster in the moment and teaches nothing, since the child experiences tidiness as something that happens to their room rather than something they do. What works better is letting the consequences occur, within reason, so that the forgotten homework is genuinely forgotten once. That's uncomfortable to watch and considerably more effective than reminding.

**6. Do you think it is important for managers to be well organized?**
> More important for managers than for almost anyone else, because the disorganisation doesn't stay contained. An individual contributor who is chaotic damages their own output, whereas a disorganised manager multiplies that across everyone who depends on them for decisions and information. The specific failure is usually not losing documents but being a bottleneck, where work stops while people wait. What I'd add is that a manager's organisation needs to be legible to others, since a private system nobody else can see is barely better than none.
