# An occasion that you lost something in public

**Part2** — Describe an occasion that you lost something in public. You should say: What you lost / When and where you lost it / What you did to find it / And explain how you felt about it

> The occasion I'd describe is losing my keys on a train. It happened a couple of years ago, on the way back from Hangzhou. It was a high-speed train, most of a day. And I'd spent a good part of it asleep, with my jacket bundled up against the window. The keys were in the jacket pocket. At some point they came out. I didn't discover this until I was standing outside my own door in Shenzhen at eleven at night. Which is the worst possible moment, because by then the train was several hundred kilometres away. What I did first was fairly useless. I went through every pocket about four times. Which everyone does, as though the keys might reappear through repetition. Then I called the station's lost property line, which does exist and is reasonably well organised. I gave them the carriage and seat number. They never did turn up. The consequences were more annoying than serious. I had to get a locksmith out at midnight, which cost roughly four times what it would have at noon. And then explain to my landlord the next morning why the lock had been changed. What I took from it was one small permanent habit. I now keep a spare set with Kai, who lives twenty minutes away. And I check my pockets before standing up on any form of transport, more or less involuntarily.

**Part3**

**1. What kinds of things do people usually lose?**
> The small things they carry without thinking about, essentially. Keys, umbrellas, water bottles, gloves, and phones, which are the most consequential and surprisingly common. The pattern is that people lose objects they handle automatically rather than deliberately. Which is why almost nobody loses a laptop and almost everybody loses an umbrella. There's a situational element too, since losses cluster around transitions, getting off a train, standing up in a restaurant, precisely the moments when attention is on the next thing.

**2. What will people do when losing things?**
> There's a fairly universal sequence. First a period of searching in places already searched, which is irrational and seems to be unavoidable. Then retracing, either physically or mentally, back through the day. Then the practical stage, contacting whoever might hold it, the restaurant, the station, the lost property office. What people do considerably less often than they should is act on the consequences quickly, cancelling a card or changing a lock. Because there's a reluctance to accept that the thing is genuinely gone.

**3. Is it effective to offer rewards to find lost things?**
> Effective for objects with high sentimental value and low resale value, which is where the incentive gap is widest. If somebody finds a wedding ring, a reward makes returning it worth the inconvenience. And the finder isn't going to profit otherwise. Where it works badly is for anything valuable in itself, since the reward has to compete with simply keeping it. There's also a documented effect where offering money can reduce returns. Because it converts a moral act into a transaction and some people find that distasteful.

**4. What kinds of people may lose things often?**
> People under cognitive load, mostly, which is a broader category than it sounds. Anyone tired, stressed, or managing several things at once is operating on habit rather than attention. And habit is where losses occur. Parents of small children are a fairly obvious example. Beyond that, people without fixed systems, in the sense that somebody whose keys always go in the same pocket will rarely lose them and somebody who puts them wherever is free will lose them regularly. It's about structure more than carelessness.

**5. What can we do to reduce the possibility of losing things?**
> Reduce the number of decisions, essentially. A fixed place for each important object means the habit does the work and attention isn't required. Beyond that, fewer things: the less you carry, the less can go. Trackers have genuinely changed this for keys and wallets, and they're inexpensive enough now to be worth it. And I'd add the practice of a deliberate check at every transition, before standing up, before leaving. Which takes three seconds and catches most of it.

**6. What do people often feel after losing things?**
> Disproportionate irritation, first, and usually directed at themselves rather than the situation, since losing something feels like evidence of a personal failing. Then anxiety about the consequences, which for a phone or a wallet is entirely rational. What's more interesting is the attachment people discover afterwards, since objects turn out to carry associations they hadn't registered until they were gone. And there's often a lingering incompleteness, because unlike a broken object, a lost one might still exist somewhere. And that's oddly difficult to close.

# A time when you saved money to buy something special

**Part2** — Describe a time when you saved money to buy something special. You should say: What the special thing was / Why you wanted to buy it / How you saved money for it / And explain how you felt after buying it

> The time I'd describe is saving up for a proper Chinese chess set, as a present for Kai. It took me about seven months. We've played since we were seven, when his grandfather taught us both. And the set Kai still uses is the one his grandfather left him. A paper board and plastic pieces from the eighties, with the characters worn off two of them completely. He'd never replace it, for obvious reasons. Why I had to save is that the set I wanted was expensive. A folding board and hand-carved boxwood pieces, from a workshop in Yunnan. Somewhere around two and a half thousand yuan. That's not an impossible sum on my salary. But Shenzhen rent has a way of absorbing whatever's left. And I'd have found a reason not to spend it. How I did it was deliberately unsophisticated. I worked out what I could set aside each month without it affecting anything visible, which was about three hundred and fifty yuan. And I moved it on the day I got paid, rather than at the end of the month. Because anything left until the end of the month doesn't exist. I also stopped buying coffee on the way into the office. Which sounds like a cliché from a personal finance book, and turned out to be nearly a third of it. What made it work, honestly, is that the goal wasn't for me. I've saved for things for myself and consistently failed. Because there's always a reasonable argument for spending it.

**Part3**

**1. Should schools teach children how to manage money from a young age?**
> Yes, and considerably earlier than most schools do it. What's teachable young is the basic relationship between time, work and money. And the fact that spending now removes an option later, which children grasp perfectly well through pocket money. The more technical content, interest, credit, how debt compounds, belongs in adolescence and is badly neglected, since a great many people encounter their first credit product with no framework for evaluating it. The strongest argument is that the alternative is learning it through an expensive mistake.

**2. Should parents teach their children to spend money freely or to save money?**
> Both, and the framing as opposites is where parents go wrong. A child taught only to save develops anxiety about spending and no sense of what money is for. And a child who spends freely never experiences the trade-off. What seems to work is giving them an amount and genuine authority over it, including the authority to waste it, since the lesson is in the consequence rather than the instruction. A child who spends everything in two days and then wants something has learnt more than one who was simply told.

**3. Do you think it is easier to teach teenagers how to manage money when they are around 16 or 17 years old?**
> Easier in the sense that they can follow the reasoning, and harder because the habits are largely formed. Sixteen is a good age for the technical content, since it's shortly before it becomes relevant and teenagers are capable of abstract reasoning about the future. But the underlying disposition, whether money feels like something to be controlled or something that disappears, is established much earlier and is mostly absorbed from watching parents. So I'd say sixteen is right for the knowledge and late for the attitude.

**4. Is it easier to spend money or save money?**
> Spending, by an enormous margin, and the asymmetry is structural rather than moral. Spending is a single pleasant decision with an immediate reward, while saving is a repeated decision with a delayed and abstract one. And everything known about how people weigh the present against the future works against it. That's precisely why automatic saving works so well, because it removes the repeated decision. The people I know who save effectively have almost all arranged it so that no willpower is involved.

**5. What kind of things do people usually save money for?**
> Property first, in China overwhelmingly so, and often with several family members contributing. Which shapes financial behaviour from a young age. Beyond that, education, for children and increasingly for themselves. Weddings, which have become extremely expensive. Travel and cars in the middle-income bracket. And a substantial precautionary category, saving against illness or job loss. Which is a larger motive here than in countries with more comprehensive social provision. And partly explains why the savings rate is so high.

**6. Should people spend time on charity, or just money?**
> Both, though they're not interchangeable and the question slightly obscures that. Money is more efficient for most causes, since an organisation can deploy it better than an untrained volunteer can deploy an afternoon. And there's a reasonable argument that someone should simply work and donate. What time provides is contact, and people who give only money tend to remain insulated from the problem in a way that limits how well they understand it. There's also an effect on the giver that writing a transfer doesn't produce.

# A change that you made recently

**Part2** — Describe a change that you made recently. You should say: What the change was / What caused the change / What you did for the change / And explain how you feel about the change

> The change I'd describe is that I stopped checking messages continuously. Now I deal with them at two fixed points in the day. It sounds trivial. And it's had a bigger effect on my work than anything else I've changed recently. Where it came from is that I copied it from Yaojin, my team lead. I'd noticed he was almost impossible to reach quickly, and completely reliable over any longer period. When I eventually asked, he explained the system. Everything goes into one list. It gets processed at eleven and at four. Outside those windows he doesn't look. What I did was start with email, which was easy, because nobody expects an instant reply there. Then about a month later I extended it to the team chat. Which was much harder. In China work arrives through the same app as your family. So switching off notifications feels close to antisocial. I told two or three people who'd be affected, and that turned out to be the necessary step. The first fortnight was genuinely unpleasant. I felt permanently anxious that something urgent was sitting unread. Almost nothing was. What I'd say now, about eight months in, is that the benefit wasn't the time spent replying. That was never much. Writing code means holding a lot of context in your head. And every interruption destroys it. I've got back somewhere between one and two hours of real work a day.

**Part3**

**1. Do you think it is good to change one’s daily routine?**
> Generally yes, though it depends which part is being changed. A routine's value is that it removes decisions. So disrupting it for its own sake wastes the thing that makes it useful. What's worth changing periodically is the content rather than the structure, since routines tend to accumulate elements that no longer serve any purpose and nobody notices because they're automatic. I'd say a routine should be examined about once a year and otherwise left alone. Because the constant optimisation of one's own habits is itself a way of avoiding work.

**2. Do you think it is good to change jobs?**
> Good when there's something specific to move towards. And considerably less so when it's motivated by dissatisfaction alone, since the dissatisfaction frequently travels. The economic case for changing is fairly strong in my industry. Because pay rises are usually larger when moving than when staying. Against that, there's a real cost to leaving just before the point where experience compounds. Chen has been at the same company eight years and is unarguably better at it than anyone who has passed through in that time.

**3. Is it good for people to get a job promotion?**
> Usually, though it's assumed to be straightforwardly good more often than it should be. A promotion typically means less of the work you were good at and more management, which is a different job requiring different skills. And a substantial number of people are made worse off by being promoted out of something they enjoyed. The financial and status gains are real. What I'd say is that it's worth treating as a change of occupation rather than as a reward. And rather few people evaluate it that way.

**4. Do people often make plans around their regular routines?**
> Constantly, and mostly without noticing. A routine defines the available slots. So plans get shaped around fixed points, meals, commutes, working hours, rather than being made freely. That's efficient, since a plan that respects the existing structure is far more likely to survive. What's less visible is how much the routine limits what gets considered at all. Because anything that doesn't fit the existing shape tends not to be proposed. And people rarely notice the options they never generated.

**5. Who tend to change their daily routine more, young people or old people?**
> Young people, by a considerable margin, though the reasons are circumstantial rather than temperamental. Young people's lives change externally, with terms ending, jobs starting, relationships forming. So their routines are disrupted whether they choose it or not. Older people's circumstances are more stable, and the routine has usually been refined over decades into something that works. There's also a physiological element, since sleep and energy become less flexible with age. Which makes irregular schedules considerably more costly.

# A time you made an important decision and were happy with the result

**Part2** — Describe a time you made an important decision and were happy with the result. You should say: What the decision was / Why you made the decision / How easy it was for you to make the decision / And explain why you were happy with the result

> The decision I'd describe is turning down a job when I graduated. Which sounds straightforward, and was the hardest thing I've decided so far. I had two offers. One from a large, well-known tech company. Paying around forty percent more. On a team maintaining an internal platform that was already built. The other from a much smaller company, where the interview had basically been an argument about how you'd structure something. And where I'd be one of four people rather than one of sixty. What made it genuinely difficult is that every external signal pointed the other way. My parents were clear about which one a sensible person takes. And they weren't being unreasonable. The name meant something and the money was real. Several classmates thought I was posturing. How I decided, in the end, was by asking what I'd actually be doing on an ordinary Tuesday in each place. Rather than how it would look on a CV. At the bigger company, the honest answer was maintaining somebody else's architecture. Why I'm happy with the result is that almost everything since has followed from it. I've had my hands on decisions I wouldn't have touched for another five years elsewhere. And what I want now, to move towards system design and work abroad for a while, came straight out of discovering that this is the part I care about. I earn less than most of my cohort. I'd choose the same again.

**Part3**

**1. How can we tell the difference between an important and an easy decision?**
> Mainly by whether it's reversible, which I find more useful than thinking about scale. A decision you can undo cheaply should be made quickly, almost regardless of how large it looks. And a decision that closes off alternatives deserves real deliberation even if it seems minor. The other marker is how far the consequences extend beyond you, since anything affecting other people's plans carries a different weight. What misleads people is emotional intensity. Because the decisions that feel agonising are often the ones where either option would have been fine.

**2. Do you think it is a good idea to make a small decision every day?**
> Yes, in the sense that deciding is a skill that atrophies without use. People who defer everything become worse at it. And they also accumulate a background of unresolved questions that takes up more attention than making the decisions would have. What I'd add is that a great many small decisions are best eliminated rather than made well. Which is the logic behind people who wear the same thing daily. The aim isn't to decide more, it's to spend the deciding on things that warrant it.

**3. What little decisions do people make every day?**
> An enormous number, most of which never register. What to eat, when to leave, which route, whether to reply now, what to look at. Estimates of the daily total are usually inflated, but the volume is clearly large. And most of it is handled by habit, which is precisely why habits matter so much. The interesting ones are the invisible decisions, choosing to check a phone rather than think, or to take the easier of two tasks. Which accumulate into something resembling a character.

**4. What if people in a group have different opinions on a decision?**
> It depends entirely on whether the disagreement is about facts or about values. If it's about facts, the answer is to establish what's actually true. And groups frequently skip that and go straight to negotiating. If it's about values or priorities, then somebody has to decide. And the least destructive arrangement is usually a clear owner who consults properly and then chooses. What fails reliably is the search for consensus. Which produces a compromise nobody supports and leaves the real disagreement intact underneath.

**5. Do you think most of people’s decisions are made in their 20s?**
> A disproportionate number of consequential ones, certainly, which is an uncomfortable feature of how life is arranged. Education, career direction, where to live and often who to live with all get settled in that decade, by people with the least information and experience they'll ever have. What I'd qualify is that these decisions are less final than they appear. And people change career and city considerably more than they used to. But the early choices do narrow what's easily available afterwards.

**6. Which is better, to make a decision quickly or slowly?**
> Quickly for anything reversible, slowly for anything that isn't, which covers most cases. The pathology in both directions is real: people who deliberate endlessly are usually avoiding the discomfort of committing. And people who decide instantly are often protecting themselves from information that would complicate things. What I'd add is that beyond a certain point more deliberation doesn't improve the decision, it just produces better justification for what was already preferred. And recognising that point is most of the skill.

# A time when you sent a message or an email to someone but received no reply for a long time

**Part2** — Describe a time when you sent a message or an email to someone but received no reply for a long time. You should say: Who you sent it to / What the message/email was about / Whether you finally received the reply / And explain how you felt about the experience

> The occasion I'd describe is a pull request I submitted to an open-source project. About eight months ago. It's a fairly widely used library that we depend on at work. I'd hit a bug in it, traced the cause, and fixed it. I wrote what I thought was a careful submission. A small change. A test that demonstrated the problem. And a short explanation of why I'd solved it that way rather than another. What happened was nothing at all. I'd expected some response within a week. And I checked, I'm slightly embarrassed to say, several times a day for the first few days. After two weeks I assumed the maintainers hadn't seen it. After a month I started constructing fairly elaborate explanations. Most of which involved something being wrong with the code itself. I reread my own diff maybe a dozen times, looking for what I'd got wrong. What I eventually did, about six weeks later, was leave one short comment asking whether anything was needed from me. And then let it go. There was still no reply. How I feel about it now is much more relaxed than I did at the time. Largely because I've since learnt how many unreviewed submissions sit in the queues of popular projects, maintained by two exhausted volunteers. What I got wrong wasn't the code. It was assuming a response was owed. And it taught me something fairly useful about how to read silence.

**Part3**

**1. In what situations do people spend a long time responding to others’ messages?**
> When the message requires a real decision rather than an acknowledgement, mostly. Anything that needs thought gets postponed to a moment that never quite arrives. Which is why difficult messages sit unanswered while trivial ones get instant replies. Beyond that, when the person is overwhelmed, since volume produces a kind of paralysis. And when the honest answer is one they'd rather not give. Because declining takes more effort than agreeing and delay postpones the discomfort. Almost none of it is about the sender.

**2. In what situations do people not respond to messages at all?**
> When the message asks for something they've decided not to give and they'd rather not say so. Which is by far the most common. Silence functions as a refusal that avoids the conversation, and both parties usually understand it. Beyond that, when there's no established relationship and no obligation, which was my situation. And when a message arrives in a stream where things genuinely get lost, which is increasingly common. The uncomfortable truth is that non-response is now a normal social signal rather than a failure.

**3. What would you do if you did not receive a reply after sending out a message?**
> One follow-up after a reasonable interval, brief and without any suggestion of grievance, and then nothing. That's what I did and I'd do the same again. The follow-up is worth sending because messages genuinely do get lost, and a second one costs almost nothing. What isn't worth doing is the third. Which converts you from someone who might be forgotten into someone who is being avoided. After that the sensible interpretation is that the answer is no. And it's better to act on that than to keep waiting.

**4. Why do some people prefer sending a message instead of making a call?**
> Because it transfers the cost to the recipient, essentially, and that's a feature rather than a rudeness. A message can be answered when convenient, whereas a call demands attention immediately regardless of what somebody is doing. There's also the record, which matters for anything with detail in it. And a message allows composition, so you can say something difficult having thought about the wording. Among younger people in China an unscheduled voice call now reads as mildly alarming. Which would have baffled my parents' generation.

**5. How do you show your respect in your messages?**
> Mostly by being brief and specific, since length is a demand on somebody's time and vagueness makes them do the work of interpreting it. Beyond that, matching the register of the relationship, and being explicit about what I'm asking for rather than hinting. With older people or anyone senior there's a greater degree of formality expected, proper greeting and closing. Which in Chinese has its own conventions. And not sending anything at eleven at night unless it genuinely can't wait, which people notice more than they say.

**6. Why do some people feel angry when others don’t reply to their message?**
> Because messaging created an expectation of near-immediate reply that nothing established, and silence against that expectation reads as deliberate. Underneath it there's usually an interpretation of status, since being unanswered feels like being judged unimportant. And that stings regardless of how reasonable the actual explanation is. There's also the ambiguity, which is the genuinely uncomfortable part. A refusal can be accepted and silence can't be resolved. So people keep re-examining it, which is exactly what I did for six weeks.

# A time you watched a famous person being interviewed

**Part2** — Describe a time you watched a famous person being interviewed. You should say: Who the famous person was / What the interview was about / When and where you watched the interview / And explain how you felt about the interview

> The interview I'd describe was with Su Bingtian, the sprinter. It was shortly after the Tokyo Olympics, where he'd run nine eight three in the hundred metres semi-final. That made him the first Chinese man to reach an Olympic final in that event. I watched it online, a few days after the race. It was a long-form interview rather than a press conference. Maybe forty minutes. What made it worth watching was how unlike a sports interview it was. He was thirty-two, which in sprinting is basically the end. And the obvious line of questioning would have been about emotion and national pride. There was some of that. But the interviewer, sensibly, spent most of the time on a technical decision he'd made in his late twenties. He changed his starting leg. What that meant in practice is that he got slower for two full seasons. He described it in almost engineering terms. Breaking his own running into components. Talking about ground contact, and the angle of the first three steps. With none of the theatrical self-belief you usually get from sprinters. He was also fairly blunt about the years when it wasn't working. What I took from it is the thing I've thought about most since. He deliberately dismantled something he was already good at. At an age when nobody would have blamed him for protecting it. I've used that as a reference point for decisions of my own, which is a strange thing to get from a sports interview.

**Part3**

**1. What kinds of people are frequently interviewed?**
> People with something to sell, in the broad sense, which covers most of it. Actors and musicians promoting something, authors, politicians. And executives, all of whom appear at moments determined by their own calendar rather than by public interest. Beyond that, people who happen to be at the centre of an event. Which is the least comfortable category, since they haven't chosen it. And a growing category of experts, who are interviewed for what they know rather than what they've done. Though usually only when something has gone wrong in their field.

**2. What kinds of things do famous people often say in an interview?**
> A remarkable amount of nothing, delivered fluently, because most of them have been trained and the incentives reward saying little. There's the gratitude sequence, thanking the team and the supporters, which is sincere and entirely predictable. There's the humility formula, attributing success to others or to luck. And there's a set of prepared lines that will be deployed regardless of the question. What's genuinely interesting is rare and usually happens when somebody is asked about a technical detail of their own work. Which is precisely what made that interview unusual.

**3. What can we do to get a job interview?**
> Make the application specific, first, since the volume of generic applications means almost none are read properly. Beyond that, the honest answer is that a substantial proportion of interviews come through people rather than through advertisements. So the useful work is usually done well before any vacancy exists. And demonstrating something rather than claiming it — in my field that means code somebody can actually read. Which cuts through a great deal of assertion.

**4. What should people do to prepare for a job interview?**
> Research the organisation properly, which sounds obvious and is done badly, since most candidates read the website and stop. Prepare specific examples rather than general claims, because an interviewer remembers a story and forgets an adjective. Rehearse aloud, which feels ridiculous and materially improves fluency. And prepare questions of your own that aren't about conditions, since what you ask reveals how you think. What I'd add is to work out beforehand what your weakest point is, because they will find it.

**5. Why do we need to attend interviews when applying for jobs?**
> Because the written application is almost uninformative about the things that determine whether somebody is any good. A CV establishes the minimum and nothing else, since it can't show how a person reasons, how they handle being wrong, or whether anyone will want to work with them for three years. That said, interviews are a fairly poor instrument too, and the research on their predictive validity is not encouraging. Structured interviews and work samples perform considerably better, which is a reasonable argument for making them less conversational.

**6. What should people wear to a job interview?**
> Slightly more formally than the role's daily norm, which is a reliable default across most industries. The reasoning is that the interview is a formal occasion regardless of how casual the workplace. And the cost of being overdressed is much smaller than the cost of appearing not to have bothered. Beyond that, whatever allows you to forget about it entirely, since anything uncomfortable or unfamiliar occupies attention you need elsewhere. The aim is not to be remembered for your clothes in either direction.

# An activity you do regularly that you think is a waste of time

**Part2** — Describe an activity you do regularly that you think is a waste of time. You should say: What it is / When you usually do it / Why you do it / And explain why you think it is a waste of time

> The activity I'd describe is scrolling short videos in bed before I sleep. I do it almost every night. And I'd defend it to nobody, including myself. How it goes is entirely predictable. I get into bed at a reasonable hour, around eleven, intending to read. Then I pick up my phone to set an alarm, which is the fatal step. I never decide to do it. I just find it already happening. Proper leisure I'd defend without hesitation. Reading, seeing people, walking. What I've tried is leaving the phone in another room. Which works completely, and which I abandon after about a week. Every time. That's the honest conclusion, I suppose. I know exactly what the solution is. I've implemented it successfully more than once. And I keep quietly undoing it.

**Part3**

**1. How do you balance life and work?**
> Imperfectly, and by rules rather than intentions. The intention to stop working at a reasonable hour never survives a busy week, whereas an arrangement does. And the only things that have worked for me are structural, processing messages at fixed times and not taking my laptop into the bedroom. What I'd say is that balance is the wrong metaphor, since it implies a steady state. In practice it's more like periodic correction, where things tilt for a few weeks and you notice and pull them back.

**2. Will you continue doing something when you are aware that it’s a waste of time?**
> Almost certainly, and I'd rather be honest about that than perform a resolution. Knowing something is a poor use of time turns out to be almost entirely disconnected from stopping. Which is worth taking seriously rather than treating as a personal failure. What actually changes behaviour is altering the environment so the thing is inconvenient. Which is why leaving the phone in another room works and willpower doesn't. The knowledge matters only insofar as it makes you willing to rearrange things.

**3. What kinds of things make people feel pressured?**
> Uncertainty more than workload, first of all. People tolerate being extremely busy if they know what's expected and believe it will end. And what corrodes them is not knowing whether the effort counts. Beyond that, having responsibility without authority, which is a reliable recipe for misery. Comparison, which social media has made continuous. And the absence of boundaries, since work now follows people home through the same device they use for everything else. So there's no longer a moment when the day is definitively over.

**4. Why do some people refuse to abide by rules?**
> Sometimes because the rule is genuinely bad, and I'd start there rather than assuming the problem is the person. Rules that serve no visible purpose, or that are applied inconsistently, generate non-compliance almost automatically. And people are fairly accurate at detecting which is which. Beyond that, when enforcement is unlikely and the personal advantage is clear, which is straightforward incentive. And occasionally for identity reasons, where breaking a rule signals independence, which is most of what adolescent rule-breaking is about.

**5. Do you think stress is important in people's lives?**
> Important in the sense of unavoidable, and useful within a fairly narrow band. Short-term pressure genuinely improves performance, which is why deadlines work and why people who never experience any tend to achieve very little. The curve turns, though, and sustained stress without recovery is straightforwardly damaging, through sleep and immune function and everything downstream of those. The distinction that matters is whether it resolves. Pressure with an endpoint is productive, and pressure without one is corrosive.

**6. Is it good to have a daily routine?**
> Yes, principally because it removes decisions. Anything requiring a fresh choice each day gets negotiated with, and a routine converts it into something that simply happens. Which is why people with consistent exercise habits rarely describe them as difficult. The risk is rigidity, where the routine becomes an end in itself and disrupting it causes disproportionate distress. What I'd aim for is a firm structure at the start and end of the day and considerable flexibility in the middle. Though I'd admit to managing roughly half of that.

Which is now an enormous shopping event and barely a festival at all.

**5. Do you think it’s a waste of money for governments to celebrate national events?**
> Not inherently, though the amount is a fair question. There's a defensible function, since shared public occasions do something for social cohesion that nothing else replicates. And a country with no collective rituals is thinner for it. The cost is also genuinely recoverable in tourism and activity. Where I'd be critical is when the spending is oriented towards international display rather than towards the people attending. And when the figures aren't published, which makes the whole question unanswerable.

**6. What festivals are the most important for people in your country?**
> Spring Festival, without any competition, and it isn't close. It's the one occasion when the obligation to return home is close to absolute. And the entire transport system reorganises around it. After that, Mid-Autumn, which carries similar family weight in a milder form. Qingming matters enormously to older generations and less to younger ones. What's interesting is that the festivals which have survived best are precisely the ones organised around family obligation rather than around celebration. Which says something about what they're actually for.

# A time when you helped someone

**Part2** — Describe a time when you helped someone. You should say: Where you helped him/her / Why you helped / How you helped / And explain how you felt about it

> The occasion I'd describe is teaching my grandmother to use her smartphone properly. Which sounds minor, and took about four months. She's in her late seventies. She'd had the phone for two years, using it only to answer calls. And in the meantime everything else in Chinese life had quietly moved onto it. She couldn't pay for anything in the market without cash. The stallholders were finding that increasingly irritating. And she'd stopped going to one of them because of it. What I did initially was the wrong thing, and I'd say that's the useful part of the story. I sat with her for an afternoon and demonstrated everything. Payments, messaging, the camera. She nodded throughout and retained nothing. Because I'd shown her rather than let her do it. And because I'd covered about fifteen things in two hours. It also became clear she wasn't confused. She was frightened of pressing something irreversible and losing money. What worked was slower and much narrower. One function at a time. She did it herself while I sat on my hands. And we repeated it the following week before adding anything new. Payments took about six weeks. I also showed her how to undo things, and how to spot a scam message. Which mattered more than anything technical. Where it stands now is that she pays for her own vegetables, sends me photographs of her roof garden, and has joined a group chat with people from the lane. What I learnt was mostly about how badly I'd been explaining things.

**Part3**

**1. Should people be very kind when they help others?**
> Kind in manner, yes, though I'd distinguish that from being effusive. What matters is not making the person feel diminished by needing help. And a great deal of well-intentioned assistance manages to do exactly that. With my grandmother, the risk throughout was treating her as incapable rather than as somebody who hadn't been taught. What I'd say is that the kindness should be in the framing rather than in the tone, since being visibly gentle with somebody can itself be a way of marking them as helpless.

**2. Should children be taught to be kind to others?**
> Certainly, though mostly by demonstration rather than instruction, since children calibrate to what adults actually do rather than what they say. What can be taught explicitly is attention, noticing when somebody needs something. Which is the part that's genuinely learnable and which most unkindness comes from lacking. What I'd avoid is teaching kindness as compliance, since a child who is kind because they'll be praised has learnt something quite different and it tends not to survive the absence of an audience.

**3. Should parents help their kids with their homework?**
> With the difficulty rather than the content, which is a distinction most parents get wrong. Explaining a concept a child is stuck on is clearly useful. And doing the work for them removes the only part that was going to teach them anything. The other consideration is emotional: a parent who becomes frustrated during homework can make a child anxious about the whole subject, and that damage lasts. Sometimes the most helpful thing is to arrange for somebody else to do it.

**4. What kind of advice should parents give to their children?**
> Advice that provides information rather than conclusions, ideally. What's useful from a parent is the thing they know that the child can't, how institutions work, what a decision will feel like in five years, what they got wrong themselves. What's less useful is a verdict, since a conclusion the child hasn't reached is rarely acted on and usually resented. The most valuable version is probably an honest account of their own mistakes. Which children find considerably more persuasive than any prescription.

**5. Should parents give children advice? Why?**
> They should, while accepting it will mostly be ignored at the time and absorbed later. The case for it is straightforward, since parents have information and pattern recognition the child simply lacks. And withholding that out of respect for autonomy is a kind of abdication. What determines whether it lands is the manner, since advice offered as an opinion can be considered and advice offered as an instruction has to be resisted. And frequency matters, because constant advice becomes background noise.

**6. Can kids provide any help to parents?**
> A great deal, and considerably more than most families allow. The obvious version is practical, and in China grandparents and parents receive a lot of help with technology from children. Which is a genuine reversal of the usual direction. Beyond that, children provide perspective, since they're not embedded in the same assumptions and will ask the question nobody else thought to. What I'd add is that being allowed to help is itself valuable to a child. Because a person who's only ever received tends to feel like a dependent rather than a member.

# A piece of local news that people are interested in

**Part2** — Describe a piece of local news that people are interested in. You should say: What it was about / Where you saw/heard it / Who was involved / And explain why people were interested in it

> The story I'd describe ran in the Shenzhen media a few years ago. It was about the clearance of Baishizhou, the largest urban village in the city. I followed it far more closely than I otherwise would have, because my friend Ming had been filming there for two years. The reporting itself was fairly standard. A redevelopment project. Something like eighty thousand residents relocated. The usual quotations from the district authority about housing quality and infrastructure. And an aerial photograph of the site. What made it locally interesting was the reaction, rather than the article. It circulated very widely here. And the comments split in a way I found instructive. A substantial number of people, mostly younger and mostly not living there, were angry about the loss. Baishizhou was where a great many people got their start in this city, because it was the only place in Nanshan you could afford when you arrived. Another group, including several who'd actually lived in those buildings, pointed out that the rooms were damp and windowless. And that nostalgia is cheap when you're not the one climbing four flights in the dark. Why people were interested is that it wasn't really a story about one neighbourhood. Almost everyone in Shenzhen has watched somewhere they knew disappear. So the article worked as a place to argue about something much larger.

**Part3**

**1. Do people read the newspaper where you live?**
> Very few in print, and the decline has been steep. Older people still buy them, and my grandfather reads one daily. But among anyone under about fifty it's close to extinct as a habit. That said, the newspapers themselves haven't disappeared, they've migrated. So a great deal of what circulates on social media originates with the same organisations. What's been lost isn't the journalism so much as the form, since a newspaper presented a curated selection including things you wouldn't have chosen, and a feed doesn't.

**2. Do people prefer local or international news?**
> Local, on the whole, and by a considerable margin in terms of engagement rather than stated preference. People will say they follow international affairs and will actually read about a road closure in their own district. Because it affects them and because they can evaluate it. International news is consumed more passively and with less confidence. The exception is anything with a national dimension, which generates far more attention here than ordinary international coverage does.

**3. Do you think it's important to have a national identity?**
> Useful rather than important, and I'd hold that fairly carefully. A shared identity makes collective action possible, allows people to accept obligations to strangers. And provides a sense of continuity that most people seem to need. Those are real goods. The difficulty is that the same mechanism works by defining who isn't included. And the historical record on where that leads is not encouraging. So I'd say the need is genuine and the form it takes matters enormously.

**4. How can people develop their national identity?**
> Through the ordinary machinery of education, language and shared reference points, most of which operates without anyone experiencing it as instruction. School history and literature do a great deal of the work, as do national media and public occasions. Beyond the official version, it's formed by food, humour and the texture of daily life. Which is why people often feel their nationality most strongly abroad. What I'd note is that the identity people actually hold is usually more local than the official version. And mine is considerably more Shenzhen than anything else — which is itself complicated, since the city is younger than my parents.
