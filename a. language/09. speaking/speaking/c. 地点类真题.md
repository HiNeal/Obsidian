# A place you have travelled to that you would like to recommend to others

**Part2** — Describe a place you have travelled to that you would like to recommend to others. You should say: What it is / Where it is / What you saw and did there / And explain why you would like to recommend it to other

> The place I'd recommend is Huizhou. It's on the coast, about two hours north-east of Shenzhen. I studied there for four years, so I know it in a way you can't from a weekend. And it's where I send people who tell me they want to get out of the city. What it is, basically, is a mid-sized Guangdong city with a very long coastline attached. The part worth going for is the eastern beaches. Xunliao Bay, and the double-moon bay further round. They're the closest genuinely good beaches to Shenzhen, and a lot less developed. What I did there, for four years, was mostly ordinary student life. But the trips I'd recommend are the ones we made at weekends. Taking a bus out to the coast. Eating seafood at places with no menu, where you just point at the tank. Walking the beach in the evening, once the day visitors had gone. There's also West Lake in the city itself, which is smaller and quieter than the famous one in Hangzhou. And more pleasant for that reason, I'd argue. Why I'd recommend it is the contrast, rather than the scenery on its own. Shenzhen is relentless. Huizhou is forty minutes on the fast train into somewhere that simply isn't. I'd add one warning, though. Avoid the public holidays completely, because half of Shenzhen has exactly the same idea.

**Part3**

**1. Where do people in your country often go for holidays?**
> It splits fairly neatly by what they're after. There's the domestic mass tourism of the big public holidays, where everyone visits the famous sites at once. Then there's the escape category — Yunnan, Guizhou, Hainan — where the point is climate and slowness. For people in Shenzhen specifically, an enormous amount of travel is short-haul: Huizhou and Shantou for the coast, Guangzhou for food. And Hong Kong for the weekend, all of which are under three hours away.

**2. What is the ideal length for a holiday?**
> About a week to ten days, for most purposes. Anything under four days is largely spent travelling and adjusting, and you return without ever having properly arrived. Beyond about two weeks, at least for me, there's a point where the novelty flattens and you start missing your own routine. Which is a slightly embarrassing thing to admit. The exception is anywhere requiring a long flight and a serious time difference, where you need the first three days just to become a functioning person again.

**3. How do people usually plan holidays?**
> Far more informally than they used to. The old pattern was a travel agency and a fixed itinerary, and that's largely collapsed except among older travellers. What's replaced it is a fairly chaotic process of watching short videos, saving places on a map app. And booking transport and accommodation separately about two weeks out. The interesting consequence is that people now arrive with a list of specific photogenic locations rather than a general sense of a place, which shapes the trip considerably.

**4. Is it important to plan a holiday?**
> Worth doing, though I'd argue against planning it tightly. The case for planning is practical: transport and accommodation are cheaper and better booked early. And popular sites now require reservations in a way they didn't. What I'd resist is the itinerary that accounts for every afternoon. Because the parts of a trip people actually remember tend to be the unplanned ones. My rule, for what it's worth, is to fix the logistics rigidly and leave the days themselves fairly open.

**5. Why do many countries try to attract people to visit?**
> Money, in the first instance, and it's a substantial amount of money. Tourism brings foreign currency, supports a lot of employment that can't be relocated abroad. And spreads income into regions with few other industries. But there's a second motive that's less often stated, which is influence. A country that people have visited and enjoyed is harder to caricature, and governments understand that perfectly well. Whether that justifies the strain on the places themselves is a separate and increasingly urgent question.

**6. How do people decide when to travel?**
> Mostly by constraint rather than preference, if we're honest. Students go when term ends, and working people go when they can get leave. Which in China means the public holidays and therefore means going when everyone else is going. The people with genuine flexibility optimise for weather or for avoiding crowds. And there's a growing group deliberately travelling in the off-season for that reason. But for most people the question isn't when they'd like to go, it's when they're allowed to.

# A noisy place you have been to

**Part2** — Describe a noisy place you have been to. You should say: Where it is / When you went there / What you did there / And explain why you feel it’s a noisy place

> The noisiest place I've been recently is a Chaoshan beef hotpot place in Nanshan. I went with about eight friends on a Friday night, a couple of months ago. It's a big place on two floors. The kind with bare tables and plastic stools, and a queue outside from about seven, which is how you know it's good. We'd gone because one of us had been promoted. So we were already fairly loud before we even arrived. What we did there was eat for about three hours, which in a hotpot place is just the normal length of time. The format is social by design. Everything cooks in one pot in the middle of the table. And Chaoshan beef has strict timings — some cuts are eight seconds, some are fifteen. So there's constant arguing about what's ready, and somebody always pulls something out too early. Why is it noisy? It's several things at once, really. There's the crowd, maybe two hundred people all talking over each other. There's the extraction fan above every table, running the whole time, which on its own would make conversation hard. There's the staff shouting order numbers across the room. And because everyone's competing with all of that, the whole room just ratchets up. So by nine o'clock you're basically shouting at someone sitting a metre away. What I'd say, though, is that the noise isn't a flaw in the experience. It is the experience.

**Part3**

**1. Do you think it is good for children to make noise?**
> Within reason, yes, and I'd defend it more strongly than most adults do. Noise is largely how young children process excitement, and an environment where they're continuously silenced teaches self-suppression rather than self-control. There's a developmental argument too, about play and vocalisation being connected. Where I'd draw the line is contexts where it genuinely harms others, hospitals, late at night in shared buildings. The distinction I'd make is between being loud and being inconsiderate. Which are not the same thing and get conflated constantly.

**2. Should children not be allowed to make noise under any circumstances?**
> No, and a blanket prohibition would be both impractical and unkind. What's reasonable is that children learn context, that a library and a playground have different rules. And that's a genuinely important social skill they acquire gradually and imperfectly. What I'd object to is the expectation that children behave like small adults in public. Which has become more common as cities have got denser. A society that can't tolerate any noise from children is one that has quietly decided children shouldn't be in public.

**3. What kinds of noises are there in our life?**
> I'd separate them by whether anyone chose them. There's the noise of activity, traffic, construction, machinery, which nobody wants but which is a by-product of a functioning city. There's social noise, conversation and music and celebration. Which is often pleasant to the people making it and irritating to everyone else. There's deliberate noise, alarms and announcements, designed to be intrusive. And there's the ambient layer people stop hearing entirely, ventilation and refrigeration, which you only notice when it stops.

**4. Which area is exposed to noise more, the city or the countryside?**
> The city, by an enormous margin, and continuously rather than intermittently. Density guarantees it, since you're within earshot of thousands of people and their vehicles at all times. What's interesting is that the countryside isn't actually silent, it's full of agricultural machinery, animals and weather. The difference is the character of it rather than the volume. Rural noise is intermittent and has a recognisable source, whereas urban noise is a constant floor that never resolves. And that seems to be what wears people down.

**5. How would people usually respond to noises in your country?**
> With considerable tolerance, on the whole, and more than in most countries I've read about. A degree of ambient noise is simply expected here. And restaurants and streets are lively in a way visitors sometimes find startling. Where it does generate conflict is residential noise. And square dancing in particular has produced genuine disputes and occasionally municipal regulation. What I've noticed is a generational split, with younger urban people considerably less tolerant than their parents. Which I suspect reflects smaller flats and thinner walls.

**6. How can people consider others' feelings when chatting in public?**
> By calibrating volume to the space rather than to the conversation, essentially. Which most people manage instinctively and some don't at all. Phone calls on public transport are the clearest failure, since you're broadcasting half a conversation that nobody can tune out. The underlying principle is fairly simple: a shared space means your default should be the quieter option. And if the topic is private, the venue is wrong. What doesn't help is the assumption that headphones solve it. They solve it for you.

# A crowded place you went to

**Part2** — Describe a crowded place you went to. You should say: Where it was / When you went there / Who you went there with / What you did there / And how you felt about it

> The place I'd describe is Dongmen, the old shopping district in Luohu. I went during the National Day holiday, about a year ago. If you wanted to design an experiment in overcrowding, you'd struggle to do better. I went with two friends, and the whole thing was my idea, which I was reminded of repeatedly over the next four hours. The plan had been pretty modest. One of them needed to buy something, and we thought we'd eat afterwards. What we hadn't accounted for is that the October holiday is when the entire country travels at once. And Dongmen is where people go when they want the version of Shenzhen that existed before the glass towers. What we actually did was move very slowly, in a large mass of people, for most of the afternoon. The streets there are narrow by Shenzhen standards. And it was dense enough that you couldn't pick your own direction. You just went where the crowd went. We gave up on the shopping entirely. Every restaurant we tried had a wait of over an hour, so we ended up eating standing at a counter somewhere. How I felt about it changed over the day. At first it was genuinely funny. Then it got oppressive. And by the end I was slightly claustrophobic and very short-tempered. What stayed with me, though, was how good-humoured everyone else seemed to be about it.

**Part3**

**1. Which cities are overcrowded in your country?**
> The obvious answers are Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou and Shenzhen. But I'd argue Shenzhen is the more telling case, because it went from three hundred thousand people to seventeen million within forty years. So the infrastructure has been permanently catching up with a population that arrived faster than anyone planned for. There's also a seasonal version, where tourist cities like Hangzhou or Xi'an are perfectly manageable in March and completely overwhelmed during the October holiday.

**2. Will there be more or less green space in cities in the future?**
> More, I think, and there's reasonable evidence for it already. The shift happened once green space stopped being seen as decoration and started being understood as infrastructure — for flooding, for temperature, for public health. Shenzhen is a reasonable example, since it has over a thousand parks and treats that as a civic achievement. The counterpressure is obviously land value, since a park generates no direct revenue and the same ground could hold apartments.

**3. Will people use bikes more or less in cities in the future?**
> More, though not in the way people imagined twenty years ago. Private bike ownership in Chinese cities collapsed and then returned in a completely different form through shared bikes. Which solved the storage and theft problems that killed it the first time. What determines the trajectory now is infrastructure rather than enthusiasm. Because people cycle where it's safe and don't where it isn't. Electric bikes complicate the picture considerably, since they've absorbed a lot of the journeys bicycles would have taken.

**4. Why do people go to crowded places?**
> Because crowds are where things are, mostly, which is a slightly circular but genuine answer. The restaurants, shops and events are concentrated where the people are, so avoiding crowds means forgoing the thing you wanted. But there's a less practical reason too. A crowded place feels alive, and a lot of people find an empty restaurant faintly unsettling. Which is why queues outside them function as advertising. There's an element of social proof in almost every decision about where to go.

**5. Do you think there are some people who enjoy crowded places?**
> Certainly, and I'd count myself among them under the right conditions. A crowd at a concert or a festival produces a collective feeling you can't get any other way. And the density is the mechanism rather than a cost of it. What separates that from Dongmen on a public holiday is agency and purpose. People enjoy crowds they've chosen to join and that are doing something together. And dislike crowds that are simply in the way.

**6. Do you think it is a good trend to have more big cities?**
> Broadly yes, with serious qualifications. The economic case is well established, since large cities are more productive per person. And they're also more efficient environmentally, because density reduces per-capita energy use and makes public transport viable. The qualification is distributional. Concentration drains talent and investment from smaller places, which then decline. And it drives housing costs to levels that price out the people the city depends on. So the trend is defensible and the management of it, in most countries, has been poor.

# A natural place in your city that you enjoy visiting

**Part2** — Describe a natural place in your city that you enjoy visiting. You should say: Where it is / What it is like / How often you go there / Who you often go there with / And explain why you enjoy visiting there

> The place I'd describe is Shenzhen Bay Park. It runs about thirteen kilometres along the coast in Nanshan, ten minutes' walk from my flat. It faces Hong Kong directly across the water. Close enough that you can see the buildings on the other side clearly on a good day. What it's like is more varied than a city park has any right to be. There's a proper path for walking and cycling the whole length. And then stretches of protected wetland where the mangroves have been left alone. Migratory birds stop there in winter. Black-faced spoonbills, which are genuinely rare. And there's a section where people stand around with serious cameras, waiting. It's obviously a designed landscape rather than a wild one, and I don't think that matters much. How often I go depends on how much work I have. Which is exactly the wrong way round, obviously. In a normal week it's two or three times, usually early evening. And I go alone, which is deliberate. Sometimes I'll walk there with Kai, though we mostly end up talking about chess. Why do I enjoy it? It comes down to something fairly simple. My life happens almost entirely on a screen. And this is the one part of my routine where nothing is asking me for a response. I've also noticed I solve problems there without trying to, which I gather is well documented.

**Part3**

**1. Do you think there should be many natural places in cities?**
> Yes, and I'd frame it as necessity rather than amenity. The public health evidence is fairly strong now, on stress, on air quality, on physical activity. And cities without accessible green space produce measurably worse outcomes. There's also a practical function, since vegetation moderates temperature and absorbs rainfall, which matters increasingly as summers intensify. The design question is more interesting than the quantity question, though. Many small parks people can reach on foot do more good than one enormous one they drive to twice a year.

**2. Do you think staying in a natural place would help change people’s mood?**
> It does, and more reliably than most interventions people try. The research is reasonably consistent on this, that even fairly short periods in a natural setting reduce measured stress and improve attention. My own experience matches it, in that I go out irritable and come back merely tired. What I'd add is that the mechanism seems to be partly about attention rather than nature as such. A natural environment is interesting enough to occupy you without demanding anything, which is a combination almost nothing else provides.

**3. Which is more important, protecting the environment or developing the economy?**
> I'd reject the framing, honestly, because the trade-off is far less real than it's presented as being. Over any serious time horizon the environment is the precondition for the economy rather than a competitor to it. And the costs of degradation arrive later and land on people who didn't benefit. Where the tension is genuine is in the short term and for poorer countries, where demanding restraint from people who haven't yet developed is difficult to justify. But framing it as a choice has mostly served those who profit from postponing it.

**4. What kind of people do you think enjoy being in nature?**
> A much wider range than the stereotype suggests, and I'd be wary of the idea that it's a personality type. The outdoors enthusiast is a fairly specific cultural figure. What's more universal is that almost everyone responds to it, and the difference is largely access and habit. People who grew up with nature nearby return to it as adults. And people who didn't often feel they're not the sort of person who does. That's a learnt belief rather than a temperament, which is why urban design matters so much here.

**5. Do you think people should spend more time in nature?**
> Yes, though I'd put the responsibility on cities rather than on individuals. Telling people to spend more time in nature is close to useless if reaching it requires a car and half a day. What changes behaviour is proximity, and Shenzhen demonstrates that fairly well — the coastal parks are heavily used precisely because people can walk to them. The benefits are real enough, on mood, sleep and attention, that I'd treat it as a public health measure rather than a matter of personal virtue.

**6. What can governments do to encourage people to spend more time in nature?**
> Build it closer, first and foremost, since distance predicts use better than any campaign. Beyond that, make it genuinely usable, meaning lit, safe after dark. And connected to where people live on foot rather than requiring a journey. Protecting what already exists from development is the unglamorous half of this and probably the more important one. Where I'd spend less is on persuasion. Because the evidence suggests people use green space in proportion to how convenient it is, almost regardless of what they've been told about it.

# A boring place

**Part2** — Describe a boring place. You should say: Where it is / Who you went there with / What you did there / And explain why you think it is a boring place

> The place I'd describe is a shopping mall in a new district in the north of Shenzhen. I went about a year ago, because a friend had moved out that way and there was nowhere else to meet. It's a big mall, maybe five floors, and it was perhaps a third occupied. What made it boring is worth being precise about. Because the obvious answer, that there was nothing to do, isn't quite right. There was plenty to do, in the sense of things to buy. What there wasn't was anything unexpected. Every shop was a branch of something I'd already seen in every other mall in this city. In the same order. With the same lighting, and the same music at the same volume on all five floors. The food court had the same eight chains. Even the empty units were empty in a uniform way, boarded off with identical panels advertising the same future tenants. We spent about three hours there. And I couldn't tell you one specific thing that happened. What struck me afterwards was the contrast with the market in the urban village near my grandmother's. That place is objectively less comfortable, noisier, and far less convenient. And something is always going on there. The mall had been designed so thoroughly that nothing could happen in it that hadn't been planned for.

**Part3**

**1. Why do most children think education is boring?**
> Largely because it's organised around examination rather than around curiosity, and a child can sense the difference immediately. When the purpose of learning something is that it will be tested, the subject becomes a means and the interest drains out of it. Beyond that, the pace is fixed for a whole room. So a substantial proportion of children are either lost or waiting. And there's a passivity problem, since sitting and receiving for six hours contradicts almost everything known about how children actually learn.

**2. Why aren’t young people willing to listen to the experiences of older people?**
> Partly because advice is usually offered as a conclusion with the reasoning removed. And a conclusion you haven't arrived at yourself is difficult to act on. There's also a genuine relevance problem, since older people's experience was acquired in circumstances that may no longer hold. And young people are often right that it doesn't transfer. And a status element: accepting advice concedes a hierarchy, which is precisely what somebody establishing independence is resisting. What works far better is a story rather than an instruction.

**3. What can people do when they feel bored?**
> The useful distinction is between filling the time and using it. Filling it, through a phone, is what most of us default to and produces nothing. Using it means letting the boredom run, which is uncomfortable and is reliably where unplanned thinking happens. Practically, I'd suggest going outside without headphones, which sounds trivial and changes what your mind does. Beyond that, boredom is often a signal rather than a problem. And it's usually indicating that whatever you're doing has stopped meaning anything.

**4. Why are some teachers’ classes boring? Are there any solutions?**
> Usually because the teacher is transmitting rather than engaging. And the difference is whether anyone in the room has to think. A class where the content is read out has no mechanism for attention. Beyond that, some subjects are taught with the interesting question removed. So students receive conclusions without ever encountering the problem that made them necessary. The solutions are known and are mostly about structure: ask questions before giving answers, make students produce something. And stop talking considerably sooner than feels comfortable.

**5. Why do some young people feel bored when talking with old people?**
> Frequently because the conversation has no shared ground, and both sides default to the topics that reveal that. Older people ask about study and plans, which is a form of care that lands as interrogation. Beyond that, there's often an asymmetry of interest, since an older person may genuinely want to know about the young person's life and the reverse isn't always true. What breaks the pattern is a specific shared activity. Which is why I get on far better with my grandmother over her balcony garden than across a dinner table.

**6. Do most people think news about celebrities is boring?**
> Most people say so and most people consume it anyway, which is the honest position. The stated objection is that it's trivial, and it obviously is. What it provides, though, is a common reference point in a culture that has very few left. And it's low-cost social currency, something to discuss with people you don't know well. There's also a less comfortable element: it's a socially sanctioned form of judging people. And the appetite for that appears to be fairly deep and not going anywhere.

# A city that you have been to and would like to visit again

**Part2** — Describe a city that you have been to and would like to visit again. You should say: When you visited it / What you did there / What it was like / And explain why you would like to visit it again

> The city I'd talk about is Hangzhou. I went about three years ago, over one of the longer public holidays. I went with two friends, mostly because none of us had been, and because the fast train makes it manageable from Shenzhen. Though it's still the best part of a day. What we did was fairly predictable, and none the worse for it. We spent most of the first two days around West Lake. I'd been sceptical about that, honestly. I'd seen the photographs a hundred times and assumed it would be a queue with scenery behind it. But walking the full circuit early in the morning turned out to be a completely different thing. We also went out to the tea villages in the hills behind the lake. That's what I'd go back for on its own. We spent an afternoon being shown how the leaves are pan-fired by hand, which I hadn't known was still done that way. What it was like is a city that's learnt to carry an enormous amount of tourism without being swallowed by it. It's also one of China's main tech centres, so there's a real working city alongside the scenery. Why I'd go back is partly that I rushed it. Four days for that city isn't enough. But the honest reason is that I went as somebody ticking off a famous place. And I'd like to go back knowing what to actually look at.

**Part3**

**1. What's the difference between the city and the countryside?**
> The obvious differences are density and pace, though I think the deeper one is about anonymity. In a city you can be unobserved. Which is liberating and isolating in roughly equal measure, whereas rural life involves being known, with everything that follows from that in both directions. Beyond that, there's the matter of choice. A city offers an enormous range of everything, work, food, people, and the countryside offers depth rather than variety. What's changed recently is that the material gap has narrowed considerably, with delivery and internet access reaching most rural areas.

**2. Some people say large cities are suitable for old people. What do you think?**
> There's a genuine case, though it cuts both ways. What large cities offer older people is medical care. Which is the single biggest factor, along with public transport and the ability to reach things without driving. Those matter enormously as mobility declines. What they offer far less of is community. And older people in big Chinese cities are often isolated in a way their rural counterparts aren't. So I'd say large cities suit older people's practical needs and undermine their social ones. And which dominates depends largely on health.

**3. Do you think it is possible that all of the population move to cities?**
> Not entirely, and I'd be wary of the direction of travel here. Urbanisation is clearly continuing and most projections have it well above eighty percent globally by mid-century. But complete urbanisation isn't coherent, since food production, energy and resource extraction all require people outside cities. What's more likely is that rural areas become less populated and more functional. There's also a countervailing trend worth noting, where remote work has allowed a small but real movement in the opposite direction.

**4. Do you think people in the countryside are friendlier than people in the city?**
> Friendlier in some respects and not in others, and the framing flatters the countryside slightly. Rural interactions are warmer with strangers, certainly, and there's a baseline of mutual assistance that cities lack. But that same closeness comes with surveillance and a fair amount of social pressure. And small communities can be brutal to anyone who doesn't fit. Urban reserve is often mistaken for coldness when it's actually a practical adaptation, since you can't have a meaningful interaction with every one of the thousands of people you pass.

**5. Are there any changes in your city?**
> Enormous ones, and at a pace that still catches me out. The metro has gone from a single line to more than a dozen since I was at school. Districts that were farmland when I was small are completely built up. Beyond the physical change, the urban villages are being redeveloped one after another, which is what my friend Ming has spent years filming. And that's changing the character of the city rather than just its appearance.

**6. What should the government do to improve citizens’ safety?**
> Design the environment rather than police it, mostly. Street lighting, sightlines and the simple presence of people doing ordinary things at night make a place safer far more reliably than enforcement does. Traffic is the underrated part of this, since road accidents kill vastly more people than crime in most cities. And that's addressed through street design and speed limits. Beyond that, I'd say emergency response times and building safety enforcement, which are unglamorous and matter enormously when they fail.

# A home that you like to visit but do not want to live in

**Part2** — Describe a home that you like to visit but do not want to live in. You should say: Where it is / What it is like / Why you like to visit it / And explain why you would not like to live there

> The home I'd describe is my grandmother's house. It's in one of the urban villages in the north of Shenzhen, where my mother grew up, and where my grandmother still lives. I've been going there my whole life. What it's like is completely unlike anywhere else I know in this city. It's a narrow four-storey building that my grandfather built himself in the eighties, before any of this was regulated. It's in a lane where the buildings are close enough that you can reach out and touch the one opposite. People here call them handshake buildings, for exactly that reason. The stairs are steep and unlit. The rooms are dark. And the flat roof has been turned into a vegetable garden. Nothing about it has changed in twenty years. Why I like visiting is partly that it runs at a completely different speed. You sit on the roof, somebody brings tea, and several hours go by without anyone proposing an activity. Neighbours come up without knocking. That happens nowhere else in my life. Why I wouldn't want to live there is more straightforward. It's dark. The damp gets into everything in the wet season. The plumbing is original. And the lane is loud until well past midnight. Beyond the practical stuff, there's basically no privacy. That openness is exactly what makes it lovely to visit. And I suspect it would suffocate me within a month.

**Part3**

**1. Do Chinese people like to visit others’ homes?**
> Very much, though the form has shifted in the last generation. Visiting was historically central, particularly around Spring Festival, when there's an established sequence of which relatives you visit on which day. What's changed is frequency and spontaneity. My parents grew up dropping in on neighbours unannounced. And that's largely disappeared in the cities, partly because everyone lives in secured buildings and partly because people now arrange things by message first. The occasions remain, but the casual version has mostly gone.

**2. What do Chinese people do when they visit others?**
> Eat, first and foremost, since a visit that doesn't involve food is barely a visit. The host will over-cater dramatically and insist that nothing has been prepared, which everyone understands as a formality. There's usually a ritual of refusing and accepting, gifts, tea, the good seat. Which foreigners find baffling and which is genuinely about demonstrating that neither party is taking the other for granted. Beyond that, a great deal of fruit gets cut and distributed, and someone will insist you take food home.

**3. What kind of place do people in your country like to live in?**
> Apartments in cities, overwhelmingly, because that's essentially what exists. Houses are rare enough to be a mark of considerable wealth. What people aspire to within that is fairly consistent: newer buildings, a good school catchment, proximity to a metro station. And a decent-sized living room, since that's where family life happens. There's also been a noticeable shift towards compounds with substantial green space and facilities. Which developers now compete on more than they compete on the flats themselves.

**4. What's the difference between homes in cities and those in the countryside?**
> Space and structure, most obviously. Rural homes are typically two or three storeys, built by the family rather than a developer, often with a courtyard and land attached. And they're considerably larger than any urban flat. The city version is a unit in a tower with shared facilities below. But the more interesting difference is how they're used. A rural house is a workplace as well as a home, with storage and drying and sometimes animals, whereas an urban flat is purely residential. That changes the whole logic of the layout.

**5. What kind of gifts do people usually bring when they visit others?**
> Food and drink, predominantly. Fruit is the standard default, and it's expected to be good fruit, presented well. Beyond that, tea or alcohol for older hosts. And something for the children if there are any, which is close to obligatory. Health products for elderly relatives have become extremely common, to the point of being a slight cliché. What matters more than the item is that it's not empty-handed and not excessive, since something too expensive creates an obligation the host then has to discharge.

**6. How often do you visit your relatives or friends?**
> Less often than I should, which I think is a fairly common answer. Relatives cluster around the festivals, so there's an intense period around Spring Festival and then relatively little in between, apart from my grandmother, who I see most weeks. Friends are more regular but less formal, and it's usually meeting somewhere rather than at anyone's home. Living with my parents has something to do with that, since inviting people over doesn't feel entirely like my decision to make.
