# 人物类

## Work or studies

![[20. resource/09. speaking/speaking/a. 基础问答真题/audio/work_or_studies.mp3]]

**1. What work do you do?**
> I'm an iOS developer at a technology company here in Shenzhen, somewhere over a thousand people. I work on our main consumer app, the part users actually touch, which means every mistake I make is extremely visible.

**2. Why did you choose to do that type of work (or that job)?**
> It followed fairly directly from what I studied, which was software engineering. The specific pull towards mobile was that you can see the result — you build something and then hold it in your hand, which server-side work never quite gives you.

**3. Do you like your job?**
> Most of it, and after seven years I think that's a reasonable endorsement. The engineering itself I genuinely enjoy. What I like rather less is the volume of meetings, which seems to expand every year regardless of anyone's stated intentions.

**4. What requirements did you need to meet to get your current job?**
> A technical degree, and by the time I moved to this one, a few years of shipped work. The interviews were the usual sequence: algorithms, then a round on how iOS actually handles memory and threading, then a long discussion of an app I'd built.

**5. Do you have any plans for your work in the next five years?**
> I'd like to move towards architecture rather than staying purely hands-on, so being responsible for how the app is structured rather than only building features. Whether that happens here or somewhere abroad I genuinely don't mind.

**6. What do you think is the most important at the moment?**
> Not letting my skills narrow, I'd say. Seven years on one platform makes you very good at a specific thing, and I'm conscious that this industry moves. So I've been deliberately learning things adjacent to what I already do.

**7. Do you want to change to another job?**
> Not urgently. I'd move for a genuinely more interesting problem rather than for money, though I'm aware that's easy to say while the job is going well. What would push me is if I stopped learning anything, and that hasn't happened yet.

**8. Do you miss being a student?**
> Parts of it. I miss having long uninterrupted stretches to go deep into something, which work never quite allows. What I don't miss is the vagueness of it, since nothing I built at university was ever used by an actual person.

**9. What technology do you use at work?**
> Swift mostly, with a fair amount of older Objective-C still sitting in the codebase, and Xcode, which every iOS developer complains about daily. Beyond that Git, and we've started leaning on AI assistants for boilerplate, which has genuinely changed the pace.

**10. Who helps you the most? And how?**
> My team lead, who everyone calls Yaojin — Chinese tech companies tend to use nicknames like that. He reviews my code properly rather than just approving it, and he'll ask why I did something a particular way rather than telling me it's wrong.

## Politeness

![[20. resource/09. speaking/speaking/a. 基础问答真题/audio/politeness.mp3]]

**1. How did you learn to be polite as a child?**
> Mainly by being corrected, constantly. My grandmother wouldn't let me touch my food until I'd greeted everyone at the table by name. It felt tedious when I was six, but it stuck.

**2. Do you think being polite is very important?**
> Yes, though not in a formal sense. It's less about saying the right phrases and more about not making other people feel awkward. That's what actually gets noticed, and what people remember afterwards.

**3. On what occasions should we be polite?**
> Realistically always, but it matters most with people who can't do anything for you. Waiters, delivery drivers, cleaners. How someone treats them tells you far more than how they behave at a dinner party.

**4. What did you do to show your politeness as a child?**
> The usual things, like greeting relatives properly, giving up my seat and holding doors. The one my parents drilled hardest was letting the elders start eating first, which in a Chinese family is close to non-negotiable.

## Friends

![[20. resource/09. speaking/speaking/a. 基础问答真题/audio/friends.mp3]]

**1. Is there a difference between where you meet friends now and where you used to meet them in the past?**
> A big one. At school we just met wherever was free, the playground or somebody's stairwell. Now it takes planning and usually costs money — a restaurant, a bar, somewhere you have to book. In Shenzhen it also means agreeing whose district to meet in.

**2. Why are some places suitable for meeting while others are not?**
> It mostly comes down to whether you can hear each other. A quiet café works because conversation is the point; a loud bar doesn't, unless avoiding conversation is the point. Seating matters more than people admit, too.

**3. Do you prefer to spend time with one friend or with a group of friends?**
> One at a time, generally. In a group the conversation stays on the surface, whereas one to one you actually get to whatever people are worried about. Groups are more fun, pairs are more useful.

**4. Would you invite friends to your home?**
> I do, now that I've got my own flat. It's usually a couple of people for dinner rather than anything large, partly because the place is small and partly because I'm a fairly limited cook.

**5. How important are friends to you?**
> Enormously, and more so as I've got older. I'm an only child, so friends have ended up filling the role siblings might have, the people you can complain to without explaining the background first.

**6. Do you often go out with your friends?**
> Most weekends, yes. Nothing elaborate, just food, a walk, occasionally a film. During exam season it stops completely, and then we all resurface at the same time looking slightly worse for wear.

**7. Where do you often meet each other?**
> Usually somewhere around Nanshan, since that's where several of us live and work. There's a Chaoshan beef hotpot place we keep going back to out of pure habit, and nobody wants to be the one who suggests changing.

**8. What do you usually do with your friends?**
> Eat, mainly. That's genuinely most of it. Beyond that we'll walk around the city with no particular destination, which sounds dull but is when the actual conversations tend to happen.

**9. Do you have a friend you have known for a long time?**
> Yes, one from primary school. We sat at the same desk for two years, then went to different universities, and we barely message for months at a time. We still pick up exactly where we left off.

## Teachers

![[20. resource/09. speaking/speaking/a. 基础问答真题/audio/teachers.mp3]]

**1. Do you like your primary school teachers more than your high school teachers?**
> Looking back, yes. Primary teachers seemed to care about who you were, whereas by high school it was almost entirely about results. That's not really their fault, though, since the system put that pressure on them too.

**2. Do you have a favorite teacher?**
> My high school maths teacher, Mr Zhang. He had this habit of putting a problem on the board that he hadn't solved either, and then working through it with us, wrong turns and all.

**3. In what way has your favourite teacher helped you?**
> Less with maths than with confidence, honestly. He'd take a wrong answer seriously and ask how I'd got there, so being wrong stopped feeling humiliating. That quietly changed how I approached every other subject.

**4. Are you still in touch with your primary school teachers?**
> Only loosely. A couple of them are in an old class group chat, so we exchange messages at New Year and that's about it. I doubt they'd pick me out of a crowd now.

**5. Do you have a teacher from your past that you still remember?**
> The same maths teacher, mostly. Also a primary school one who used to read to us for the last ten minutes on Fridays. I remember the ritual far better than anything she actually taught.

**6. Do you want to be a teacher in the future?**
> Probably not. I've tutored a bit and enjoyed the explaining part, but holding the attention of forty teenagers is a completely different skill, and I don't think I have the patience for it.

## Computers/tablets

![[20. resource/09. speaking/speaking/a. 基础问答真题/audio/computers_tablets.mp3]]

**1. Would you like to use a computer or tablet?**
> A laptop, for anything that counts as work. Tablets are lovely for reading on a sofa, but the moment I need to type more than a sentence I find them genuinely frustrating.

**2. In what conditions would you use a computer or tablet?**
> The laptop comes out whenever I'm writing or working with data, so essentially every weekday. The tablet only appears on trains and in bed, when I want something lighter to hold.

**3. When was the first time you used a computer or tablet?**
> I'd have been about seven, on my father's enormous desktop. I was supposed to be learning to type and spent the entire time playing a racing game, which he pretended not to notice.

**4. What would your life be like without computers or tablets?**
> My job would be literally impossible without one, so that's not really a hypothetical. What I'd actually gain is my evenings, since a fair amount of the time I spend on a screen after work isn't work and isn't restful either.

**5. In what conditions would it be difficult for you to use a computer or tablet?**
> On a crowded train, mainly, where there's nowhere to put it and somebody's reading over your shoulder. Bright sunlight is the other one, since you end up squinting at your own reflection.

## Watch

![[20. resource/09. speaking/speaking/a. 基础问答真题/audio/watch.mp3]]

**1. Do you wear a watch?**
> Every day, though it's a cheap one. It's less about telling the time than about having a reason not to pull my phone out, because once the phone's in my hand I've lost twenty minutes.

**2. Have you ever got a watch as a gift?**
> Yes, from my parents when I finished high school. It's the one I still wear, and I suspect the point was partly symbolic, something about taking my own time seriously from then on.

**3. Why do some people wear expensive watches?**
> Rarely for the timekeeping, I'd imagine. It's a signal, and quite a subtle one compared with a car. There's genuine craftsmanship involved too, so some people collect them the way others collect cameras.

**4. Do you think it is important to wear a watch? Why?**
> Not important, but useful. Everyone has the time on their phone, so a watch is a convenience rather than a necessity. What it does do is let you check without appearing rude mid-conversation.

## Paper

![[20. resource/09. speaking/speaking/a. 基础问答真题/audio/paper.mp3]]

**1. Have you made any crafts with paper?**
> Only the basics, folding cranes and paper planes as a child. My cousin is genuinely good at origami and once made a dragon that took her an entire afternoon, which rather put my efforts in perspective.

**2. What did you like to do with paper as a child?**
> Draw on it, mostly, and then fold whatever I'd drawn into something else. I also went through a phase of making elaborate paper fortune tellers, which every child in my class seemed to catch at once.

**3. Do you still write physical letters?**
> Almost never, which is a slight shame. I write cards at New Year and that's about the extent of it. Everything else has migrated to WeChat, where it gets read and forgotten within seconds.

**4. Do people still keep handwritten letters today?**
> The ones who receive them do, I think, precisely because they're rare now. Nobody prints out messages, but a letter tends to end up in a drawer somewhere and stay there for decades.

**5. Do you carry paper and pens with you when you go out?**
> A small notebook, yes, more out of habit than necessity. I find I remember things better if I write them by hand, even though I could type the same note in half the time.

## Fruit and vegetables

![[20. resource/09. speaking/speaking/a. 基础问答真题/audio/fruit_and_vegetables.mp3]]

**1. How often do you eat fruit and vegetables?**
> Vegetables most days, since it's hard to eat Cantonese food without them. Fruit is more erratic and depends entirely on whether I've remembered to order any, which living alone means nobody else is going to do for me.

**2. Where do you usually buy fruit and vegetables?**
> Mostly through a delivery app, which in Shenzhen arrives within about half an hour and is how almost everyone my age shops now. When I visit my parents my mother still goes to a proper market and is quietly disapproving about this.

**3. What kind of fruits and vegetables do you dislike?**
> Coriander, emphatically, which I know divides people along almost genetic lines. Durian too, though I'll admit that's about the smell rather than any serious attempt at eating it.

**4. Were there any kind of fruits and vegetables you disliked as a child?**
> Bitter melon, which I found genuinely unbearable. My grandmother insisted it was good for me and cooked it anyway, and somewhere in my twenties I started ordering it voluntarily, which I still can't explain.

**5. Do people in your country like planting vegetables?**
> Enormously, and often in improbable places. Older people in particular will turn a balcony or any spare patch of ground into a vegetable plot, and they'll grow more spring onions than any household could use.

## Shoes

![[20. resource/09. speaking/speaking/a. 基础问答真题/audio/shoes.mp3]]

**1. Do you like buying shoes? How often?**
> Not especially, and only when a pair wears out, so perhaps twice a year. I'd rather own three pairs I can walk in all day than a wardrobe of things I'm saving for an occasion.

**2. Have you ever bought shoes online?**
> A few times, and it's roughly a coin flip. Sizes vary so much between brands that I now only order online if I've tried that exact model in a shop first.

**3. How much money do you usually spend on shoes?**
> Somewhere between three and five hundred yuan, normally. I'll go higher for something I'll walk in every day, on the logic that cheap pairs fall apart and end up costing more.

**4. Which do you prefer, fashionable shoes or comfortable shoes?**
> Comfortable, without much hesitation. I walk a lot, and a beautiful pair that leaves you limping by lunchtime isn't really a good pair of shoes. The best ones manage both, obviously, but they're not cheap.

## Cars

![[20. resource/09. speaking/speaking/a. 基础问答真题/audio/cars.mp3]]

**1. Will you buy an expensive car in the future?**
> I doubt it. In a city like mine a car is mostly a parking problem with wheels, so if I ever buy one it'll be something modest and practical rather than anything worth admiring.

**2. Do you think car colours are important?**
> More than I'd expected, apparently. People say black looks serious and white shows every mark, and there's a whole folklore around resale value. Personally I'd pick whichever one hides dirt best.

**3. Did you enjoy traveling by car when you were a kid?**
> I loved it. My uncle would drive us out to the countryside at weekends and I'd sit in the back with the window down the whole way. It's the smell of those trips I remember most.

**4. What types of cars do you like?**
> Small ones, honestly. Anything I could park without a three-point turn in a narrow lane. I've never understood the appeal of an enormous SUV in a city where the streets weren't built for them.

**5. Do you prefer to be a driver or a passenger?**
> Passenger, easily — though in practice I'm neither, since I don't own a car. Driving in Shenzhen traffic is forty minutes of concentration for no reward, whereas on the metro you can read, sleep or just watch the city go past.

**6. What do you usually do when there is a traffic jam?**
> Put a podcast on and accept it. Getting irritated changes nothing and just arrives with you. If it's serious I'll abandon the car altogether and take the metro, which ignores traffic entirely.

## Secondary school

![[20. resource/09. speaking/speaking/a. 基础问答真题/audio/secondary_school.mp3]]

**1. Do you remember your first day at secondary school?**
> Vividly, mostly the discomfort of it. New uniform, nobody I knew, and a seating plan that put me next to a boy who didn't speak for a week. We're still in touch, oddly enough.

**2. Were there any subjects that you found difficult at secondary school?**
> Physics, consistently. I could follow every step in class and then find myself completely lost the moment a problem was phrased differently, which I've since learnt means I never really understood it.

**3. What was your favourite subject at secondary school?**
> Maths, largely because of the teacher. He treated wrong answers as interesting rather than embarrassing, and that made the whole subject feel like something you could poke at rather than obey.

**4. Is there anything you miss about your secondary school?**
> The friendships, mainly, and how little effort they took. You saw the same forty people every day for three years, so closeness happened by default rather than by scheduling it a week ahead.

## Rubbish and recycling

![[20. resource/09. speaking/speaking/a. 基础问答真题/audio/rubbish_and_recycling.mp3]]

**1. Is waste sorting common in your country?**
> It's become far more common in the big cities over the last few years, partly because several of them made it compulsory. Enforcement varies enormously, though, and older residents took some persuading.

**2. Did you recycle when you were a kid?**
> In a fairly informal way, yes. My grandmother saved every cardboard box and plastic bottle and sold them to a collector who came round on a tricycle. That was recycling, though nobody called it that.

**3. What do you do when you see rubbish on the street?**
> If it's next to a bin I'll pick it up, and otherwise I walk past, which I'm not proud of. What actually changes things is how often the bins are emptied rather than individual conscience.

**4. How do you recycle things like paper and plastic?**
> We separate them at home into the colour-coded bins in our compound. Anything bulky, like cardboard from deliveries, I flatten and leave beside the recycling point rather than forcing it in.

## Websites

![[20. resource/09. speaking/speaking/a. 基础问答真题/audio/websites.mp3]]

**1. Do you prefer getting information from websites or books?**
> Websites for anything current, books for anything I want to genuinely understand. A search gives you the answer in ten seconds, but a book makes you sit with the argument, which is a different thing.

**2. Would you like to have your own website?**
> I've thought about it, mostly as somewhere to put things I've written. What stops me is the suspicion that I'd spend a weekend designing it and then never post anything.

**3. What kinds of websites do you often visit?**
> News in the morning, then documentation and Stack Overflow for most of the working day, which is unglamorous but accurate. In the evening it's video sites, and that's where the hours quietly disappear.

**4. What is your favourite website?**
> Bilibili, probably, though it functions more like television for me. There's an enormous amount of amateur teaching on there, and some of it explains a technical concept better than the official documentation manages.

**5. Are there any changes to the websites you often visit?**
> They've all become more like apps, and more insistent about it. Nearly every site now nags you to download something or log in before it'll show you anything, which has made the open web feel narrower.

**6. What kinds of websites are popular in your country?**
> Short-video platforms dominate, alongside shopping and social apps. What's distinctive here is how much is bundled together, so one app might handle your messages, your payments and your food order.

**7. What have you learned from websites that help with your life or studies?**
> Practical things, mostly. A good deal of what I use at work daily I picked up from online tutorials rather than at university, and I've learnt most of my cooking the same way. Nobody sat me down and taught me either.

**4. Do you listen to music while doing other things?**
> Constantly, though not while reading anything difficult. Anything with lyrics competes for the same part of my brain, so for serious work it's either instrumental or nothing at all.

## Advertisement

![[20. resource/09. speaking/speaking/a. 基础问答真题/audio/advertisement.mp3]]

**1. Have you ever bought something because of an advertisement?**
> More often than I'd care to admit. The last time was a pair of headphones I'd seen reviewed by someone I follow, which is advertising even if it doesn't announce itself as such.

**2. Do you often see advertisements when you are on your phone or computer?**
> Constantly, and increasingly hard to distinguish from real content. Half my video feed is sponsored in some way, and the ones that work best are precisely the ones I don't register as ads.

**3. Is there an advertisement that made an impression on you when you were a child?**
> There was a milk commercial with a jingle every child in my school could sing. I couldn't tell you a single thing about the product now, but I could still hum the tune perfectly.

**4. Do you see a lot of advertising on trains or other transport?**

**5. Do you like advertisements?**
> As a rule no, though I'll make exceptions for the clever ones. What I object to is the volume rather than the concept, since there's now barely anywhere you can look without being sold something.

**6. What kind of advertising do you like?**
> The ones that tell a short story and only mention the product at the very end. Thai commercials are famous for it. They earn your attention rather than interrupting whatever you were doing.

## Clothing

**1. Do you wear different styles of clothes on weekdays and weekends?**
> Slightly, though the gap is narrower than it used to be. On weekdays I'll make an effort if I'm going into the office, and at weekends I default to whatever is nearest the top of the pile.

**2. What color clothes do you like?**
> Fairly muted things, navy and grey mostly, which makes getting dressed an easier decision. Everything matches everything else, so I never have to think about it before nine in the morning.

**3. What kind of clothes do you like to wear?**
> Simple and unstructured, essentially. A jumper, jeans, trainers I can walk in. I've never had the nerve for anything bold, and I'd feel as though I were wearing a costume.

**4. Do you prefer to wear comfortable and casual clothes or smart clothes?**
> Casual, nine days out of ten. I do enjoy the occasional excuse to dress properly, though, since it changes how you carry yourself. It's just not something I'd want every day.

**5. Do you like wearing T-shirts?**
> They're most of my wardrobe for about eight months of the year, so evidently yes. Shenzhen summers are humid enough that anything heavier becomes unbearable by mid-morning, so it's less a preference than a surrender.

**6. Do you spend a lot of time choosing clothes?**
> Almost none, deliberately. I've narrowed things down to a few combinations that work, which sounds joyless but saves a surprising amount of energy for decisions that actually matter.

## Science

![[20. resource/09. speaking/speaking/a. 基础问答真题/audio/science.mp3]]

**1. Do you like science?**
> I do, though more as a reader than a practitioner. I enjoy having things explained to me and I'm hopeless in a laboratory, which is roughly why I ended up building software instead.

**2. When did you start to learn about science?**
> Formally, around the age of eight, with a general science class that was mostly about plants. The part that stuck was a documentary my father put on about space, which I watched repeatedly.

**3. Which science subject is interesting to you?**
> Astronomy, easily, though I've never studied it properly. There's something about the scale of it that resets your sense of proportion, which is oddly comforting when you're panicking about an exam.

**4. What kinds of interesting things have you done with science?**
> Nothing dramatic. We grew crystals in a jar at primary school and I once built a very poor model volcano. What I do now is engineering rather than science, which is a different discipline and considerably less photogenic.

**5. Do you like watching science TV programs?**
> Very much, particularly the natural history ones. A good documentary explains something you didn't know you were curious about, and I'd rather watch that than most dramas.

**6. Do Chinese people often visit science museums?**
> Families with children do, certainly, especially at weekends and during the holidays. The newer ones are genuinely well designed and hands-on, so they've become somewhere you'd take a child rather than somewhere you'd endure.

## Morning time

![[20. resource/09. speaking/speaking/a. 基础问答真题/audio/morning_time.mp3]]

**1. Do you like getting up early in the morning?**
> I like having got up early, which isn't quite the same thing. The first ten minutes are unpleasant every single time, but a morning where I'm up by seven feels twice as long.

**2. What do you usually do in the morning?**
> Coffee first, then whatever needs actual thinking, because that's when my head is clearest. Breakfast tends to happen late and standing up, which my mother disapproves of quite loudly.

**3. What did you do in the morning when you were little? Why?**
> Rushed, mostly. My mother would wake me, I'd eat something in five minutes and we'd half-run to school. There was no leisurely version of the morning until I got to university.

**4. Are there any differences between what you do in the morning now and what you did in the past?**
> The main one is control. As a child the morning happened to me on somebody else's schedule, whereas now I decide when it starts, which also means I have nobody else to blame.

**5. Do you spend your mornings doing the same things on both weekends and weekdays? Why?**
> Not at all. Weekday mornings run on rails because they have to, whereas at weekends I'll deliberately let them drift, since a slow morning is most of what makes a weekend feel different.

## Outer space and stars

![[20. resource/09. speaking/speaking/a. 基础问答真题/audio/outer_space_and_stars.mp3]]

**1. Have you ever learnt about outer space and stars?**
> Only at school and through documentaries, so nothing rigorous. I can name the planets and recognise a couple of constellations, which in a city with this much light pollution is about as far as it goes.

**2. Do you like science fiction movies? Why?**
> I do, the quieter ones especially. What appeals isn't the spectacle so much as the thought experiment, taking one thing that isn't true yet and following it through to its conclusion.

**3. Do you want to know more about outer space?**
> Definitely, though in a fairly undemanding way. I'd happily watch a whole series about it, but I'm not about to teach myself the physics, which is presumably the difference between interest and commitment.

**4. Do you want to go into outer space in the future?**
> Honestly, no. I admire the people who do enormously, but I'm uneasy on a long flight, so the idea of being strapped into a rocket holds very little appeal.

**2. Do you use headphones?**

**5. In what conditions would you not use headphones?**

## Opportunities

**1. Did you have any opportunities to take part in new activities as a child?**
> A reasonable number, though most were chosen for me. Piano, calligraphy, a brief and unsuccessful attempt at swimming. What I lacked wasn't opportunity so much as any say in which ones.

**2. Should people try as many different things as they can?**

## Social media

![[20. resource/09. speaking/speaking/a. 基础问答真题/audio/social_media.mp3]]

**1. Have you ever posted anything on social media?**
> Less and less. I used to post fairly freely at school, and now it's a handful of photos a year, mostly from trips. Somewhere along the way I became more self-conscious about it.

**2. When did you start using social media?**
> Around thirteen, on QQ, which everyone in my class was on. WeChat came later and effectively replaced it, though I still have that old account with some deeply embarrassing posts on it.

**3. Do you think you spend too much time on social media?**
> Certainly, and I have the screen-time figures to prove it. What bothers me isn't the total so much as the fact that I never decide to open it, I just find it already open.

**4. Do your friends use social media?**

## Hometown

**2. Did you learn about the culture of your hometown in your childhood?**
> That's a complicated question here. The local Cantonese and Hakka culture was there, and I got some of it through my grandparents. But the city's actual culture was being assembled in real time by people arriving from everywhere else, so it's a mixture rather than an inheritance.

> Very big now, around seventeen million. What's strange is that it wasn't when I was small. I can remember areas that were still fields, and those are now districts with their own metro stations, which is a peculiar thing to have watched happen.

**5. Do you like your hometown?**
> I do, though being from here gives me an unusual relationship with it. What I appreciate most is that nobody is really an outsider, because almost everyone arrived recently, so it's one of the easier cities in China to be new in.

**15. Is your hometown a good place for young people to pursue their careers?**
> One of the best in the country, particularly in technology and manufacturing. What's changed is that it's no longer cheap, so the calculation is harder than it was for the people who arrived in the nineties. The opportunities are still here, the affordability isn't.

> It is — I was born here, my household registration is here, and my family are all here, which makes me fairly unusual in this city. Whether it stays permanent depends mostly on whether I spend a few years working abroad.
