# A way/change that helps you save a lot of time

**Part2** — Describe a way/change that helps you save a lot of time. You should say: What it is / How you implement it / How difficult it is / And explain how you feel about the way/change

> The change I'd describe is a small habit I copied from Yaojin, my team lead. And it's probably saved me more time than anything else I've tried. What he does is deal with messages and tasks at two fixed points in the day, instead of continuously. Everything that comes in goes onto one list. Then it gets handled at eleven and at four. Outside those windows, he simply doesn't look. I noticed this because it made him infuriating to reach quickly, and extremely reliable over any longer period. I started doing it about a year ago. Just with email at first, because nobody expects an instant reply there. Then I extended it to the team chat, which was much harder. The mechanics are nothing special. One list, two processing windows, notifications off in between. What was difficult wasn't the system. It was the first fortnight, where I felt permanently anxious that something urgent was sitting unanswered. Almost nothing was. Why does it save so much time? It isn't really the minutes spent replying, which were never the problem. It's that programming means holding a lot of context in your head at once. And every interruption destroys that. It costs you ten or fifteen minutes to rebuild it. So if you're interrupted twenty times a day, the arithmetic is pretty brutal. I'd say I've got back somewhere between one and two hours of real work a day. The odd part is that I'd read this advice several times and ignored it. It only worked once I'd watched somebody actually live by it.

**Part3**

**1. Is it necessary for children to learn time management?**
> Yes, though I'd distinguish it sharply from the version usually taught. Handing a nine-year-old a planner and a set of priorities is largely theatre. What children can genuinely learn is the relationship between effort and time, that a piece of work takes roughly as long as it takes and can't be compressed at the last minute. That's learnt by experiencing the consequence once rather than by instruction. The more sophisticated skills, sequencing and prioritising, are probably better introduced in adolescence when there's actually something to prioritise.

**2. What can schools do to help students learn time management?**
> Stop scheduling every hour of their day, for one, which sounds counterintuitive. A student whose time is entirely allocated by adults never practises allocating it. So they arrive at university with no experience of the skill and predictable results. Beyond that, schools could be considerably more explicit about how long things actually take, since students routinely underestimate by a factor of two. Setting longer projects with intermediate deadlines is probably the single most effective intervention. Because it teaches planning backwards from an endpoint.

**3. What can people do to save time?**
> The biggest gains are usually structural rather than behavioural. Batching similar tasks, reducing the number of times you switch context, and eliminating recurring decisions by making them once. Beyond that, the unglamorous answer is saying no to things, which saves more time than any efficiency technique. What I'd warn against is the pursuit of optimisation for its own sake, since it's entirely possible to spend more time designing a system than the system will ever recover.

**4. Does technology help people save time? How and why?**
> Both, and the net effect is genuinely ambiguous. It's collapsed the time cost of specific tasks. And anyone who has watched their parents queue at a bank appreciates that. But the time saved rarely becomes free time, it becomes capacity for more tasks. And expectations adjust upwards to absorb it. Email is the standard example: it made correspondence almost instant and produced a volume of correspondence nobody had before. So technology saves time per task and adds tasks, and for most people those roughly cancel.

**5. Do you think parents should be responsible for teaching children to save time?**
> Partly, though I'd put the emphasis on demonstration rather than teaching. Children absorb their sense of how time works from watching the adults around them. And a household that's permanently late produces a particular relationship with punctuality. What parents can do deliberately is allow the natural consequences to occur. Which means occasionally letting a child be late rather than rescuing them. The instinct to prevent that is understandable and removes the only feedback the child was going to get.

**6. Do people who can manage time well become successful more easily?**
> More easily, though I'd be careful about the causation. Time management is a multiplier rather than an engine, so it amplifies whatever capability and opportunity somebody already has. There are plenty of superbly organised people in unremarkable positions. What it reliably does is prevent a particular kind of failure, the missed deadline and the forgotten commitment. And avoiding those over ten years compounds into a reputation that opens doors. So it's less that it causes success than that its absence quietly caps it.

# A gift you would like to buy for your friend

**Part2** — Describe a gift you would like to buy for your friend. You should say: How you knew your friend / How long you have known him/her / How you would choose a gift for him/her / And explain why you would choose that gift for him/her

> The gift I'd like to buy is a proper Chinese chess set, for Kai, my closest friend. We've played against each other since we were seven. His grandfather taught us both, on a folding table outside their building. And Kai has been better than me since about the second year. The set he uses is the one his grandfather left him. And that's exactly the problem. It's a paper board and a box of plastic pieces from the eighties. The printing has worn off two of them completely, so you just have to remember which is which. He'd never replace it, obviously, because of who it came from. So what I'd buy is a proper set. A folding wooden board, and boxwood pieces with the characters carved rather than printed, which is the distinction that actually matters. The good ones are made in Yunnan, and they cost a fair amount, which is why I'd need to plan for it. Why this particular gift? Because it solves a problem he won't solve himself. And I think that's the test of a good present. Anything he'd have bought on his own isn't really a gift. It's just arriving a bit earlier. And it doesn't compete with the old set, because he'd obviously keep that. What I'd want to avoid is any suggestion that I'm improving on his grandfather. So I suspect I'd hand it over with very little ceremony and let him work out what it's for.

**Part3**

**1. When do people normally send gifts to others?**
> The fixed occasions account for most of it, birthdays, weddings. And the festivals, which in China means Spring Festival above all. Beyond those, there's a substantial category around obligation, visiting somebody's home, thanking somebody who has helped you, or maintaining a professional relationship. And those are governed by fairly precise unwritten rules about value. What's grown recently is the spontaneous gift, largely because sending something online takes thirty seconds. And that's created a new and lower-stakes category that didn't previously exist.

**2. Is it hard to choose a gift?**
> Genuinely difficult, and I think the difficulty is misunderstood. People treat it as a problem of knowing the person, when it's more often a problem of knowing what they haven't already bought. Anyone with disposable income has purchased everything they actively wanted. Which leaves you searching for something they'd like and hadn't considered. That's a much narrower target. The gifts that work tend to be things somebody wants but feels unable to justify. Which requires knowing not just their taste but their sense of what's permissible.

**3. Will people feel happy when receiving an expensive gift?**
> Often less than you'd expect, and sometimes considerably less. An expensive gift creates an obligation, and the recipient immediately begins calculating what they now owe. Which is not a pleasant response to a present. It can also communicate a misreading of the relationship. What reliably produces happiness is evidence of attention, something that demonstrates the giver was paying attention over time. And that's entirely uncorrelated with price. The best gift I've received cost almost nothing and required somebody to have remembered a passing remark.

**4. Which do people give on traditional festivals, red envelopes or other kinds of gifts?**
> Red envelopes dominate, particularly between generations and particularly at Spring Festival, and their advantage is honesty. They avoid the waste of giving somebody something they don't want, and the amounts are socially legible. Physical gifts are more common between friends and among younger people, where the personal element matters more than the value. There's also a middle category that's become enormous, which is food and health products, functioning as something between the two: obviously a gift, obviously consumable. And priced clearly enough that everyone understands the register.

**5. What do people usually consider when buying gifts?**
> The relationship, first, which sets the acceptable range, and getting that wrong in either direction is the most common failure. Then practicality, since most people are wary of giving something that will sit unused. Price is considered more carefully than anyone admits. Because it's read as a statement about how much the relationship is worth. And in China specifically, there's a layer of symbolic checking, making sure the item doesn't carry an unfortunate association. Which is a genuine constraint rather than a superstition people have abandoned.

**6. Are there any unlucky things that people should not buy as gifts in your country?**
> Several, and they're taken more seriously than outsiders expect. Clocks are the classic one, because giving a clock sounds close to attending somebody's funeral. And that's close to unforgivable for an older person. Umbrellas and pears both sound like words for separation, so they're avoided between couples and friends. Sharp objects suggest cutting a relationship, and shoes imply you'd like the person to walk away. Green hats are a category of their own and I'd rather not explain that one.

# A course that impressed you a lot

**Part2** — Describe a course that impressed you a lot. You should say: What the course was about / Where you took the course / What you did during the course / And explain why it impressed you a lot

> The course I'd describe is the operating systems module I took in my second year at university. It impressed me because it was the first course that changed how I thought, rather than just what I knew. It was taught by a lecturer who'd spent about ten years writing systems software before he came back to teaching. And that turned out to matter enormously. Most of our programming courses until then had been about making something work. This one was about what was happening underneath. The structure was unusual, too. The assignments weren't exercises, they were implementations. We wrote a scheduler. Then a memory allocator. Then a small file system. Each one a simplified version of something real. And every week started with a demonstration of something breaking. A deadlock, or a race condition. Only afterwards would he introduce the mechanism that prevents it. So each concept arrived as a solution to a problem we'd already watched cause damage. What impressed me was how well it transferred. I've now got a rough mental model of what a machine is actually doing when my code runs. Which sounds obvious, and is genuinely rare among people who learnt to program from tutorials. It's also the reason I could answer anything at all in the system design round of my job interview. And it's the one course whose notes I've kept, which is probably the most honest measure there is.

**Part3**

**1. What do people need to remember in their daily lives?**
> Far less than they used to, and I think that's the interesting part of the question. The mechanical load, phone numbers and directions and appointments, has been almost entirely offloaded onto devices. And I'd argue that's a straightforward gain. What still has to be held internally is anything that needs to be available while you're thinking, since you can't look up a concept fast enough to use it in an argument. And the social layer, people's names and circumstances. Which can technically be stored and which everybody recognises it would be insulting to consult.

**2. What can we learn from history?**
> The most useful thing isn't a set of lessons, it's a sense of contingency, that arrangements which feel permanent were built by people and can be dismantled. That's the corrective to assuming the present is natural. Beyond that, there's genuine value in seeing how often confident predictions failed. Which should induce a healthy scepticism about confident predictions now. What I'd resist is the version where history supplies neat instructions, since the situations are never analogous enough for that and the lesson usually gets chosen to fit the argument.

**3. Why do some people have a better memory than others?**
> Partly innate variation, which is real but smaller than people assume, and largely a matter of technique and engagement. The consistent finding is that memory works by connection, so people who already know a great deal about a subject remember new facts about it far more easily. Which produces a compounding effect that looks like talent. Beyond that, the people with good memories are usually doing something, elaborating or testing themselves, rather than simply exposing themselves to material repeatedly.

**4. Do people like things of memorial significance?**
> Enormously, and I'd say it's close to universal. Almost everyone keeps objects with no practical function and no resale value, purely because they're attached to a moment. What's interesting is that the objects are usually unremarkable, a ticket or a worn-out jumper, rather than anything designed to be commemorative. Purpose-made souvenirs work far less well. Which suggests the attachment comes from the object having genuinely been present rather than from it representing something.

**5. Which can help people remember things better, words or photos?**
> They do different jobs, and the comparison is slightly unfair to words. Photographs are far better at preserving the surface of a moment, and they trigger recall efficiently. What they're bad at is meaning, since a photograph of an afternoon tells you nothing about why it mattered. And there's evidence that photographing something can actually weaken your memory of experiencing it. Words are worse at detail and considerably better at significance. A sentence written that evening will still explain why the day counted, which no image does.

**6. Can technology help people remember things better? How?**
> Substantially, though the effect is uneven. For storage and retrieval it's transformative, and anyone who has searched their own photographs for a date understands that. Spaced repetition software is genuinely effective for anything requiring memorisation. And it's a case of technology working with how memory operates rather than around it. Where the effect reverses is when the availability of the information removes the incentive to learn it, which is a real phenomenon. Most of us have surrendered the ability to navigate our own cities.

# A story/book with animals in it

**Part2** — Describe a story/book with animals in it. You should say: What animals are in it / What the story/book is about / Why you read the story/book / And explain what you think of this story/book

> The book I'd talk about is Wolf Totem, by Jiang Rong. I read it when I was about seventeen, at an age when I was mostly reading whatever was set for me. It's a novel, though it's closely based on the author's own experience. It's about a student from Beijing who gets sent to Inner Mongolia during the Cultural Revolution. And it's essentially about wolves. What happens is that this young man arrives with all the assumptions of a city person. He becomes fascinated by the wolves on the grassland, and eventually raises a wolf cub in secret. Around that there's a much bigger story about the herders. They've lived alongside the wolves for centuries and they understand the ecology of the place in a way the arriving officials very clearly do not. The wolves get killed off systematically. The grassland gets converted to farmland. And it turns into desert within a generation. What made it stay with me is that the animals aren't symbolic in any comfortable way. The wolves are genuinely dangerous. They kill livestock. The book doesn't sentimentalise them at all. The argument is ecological rather than emotional. The wolves were load-bearing, and removing them collapsed the whole system. I'd also say it was the first book that made me suspicious of confident modernisation. Which wasn't a position anyone had put to me at that age. I've reread it twice since. The prose isn't especially fine, and it doesn't matter, because the thing it's describing is genuinely large.

**Part3**

**1. Should schools teach children about animals?**
> Certainly, though I'd want it taught as ecology rather than as a parade of charismatic species. Children can absorb the idea of a system, that removing one thing has consequences elsewhere, remarkably early. And that's the concept that transfers. The standard approach, which is essentially facts about interesting animals, produces affection without understanding. There's also a straightforward case about empathy, since how children learn to treat animals turns out to predict quite a lot about other things.

**2. Some people think pets should not be kept in cities. What do you think?**
> I'd disagree, though the concerns behind it are reasonable. Keeping a large dog in a small flat with no exercise is genuinely unkind. And irresponsible ownership creates real problems, noise, waste and occasionally danger. But those are arguments about how rather than whether. The benefits to owners, particularly to isolated older people, are well documented and substantial. And cities are where isolation is worst. The sensible position is regulation, on numbers, breeds and public behaviour, rather than prohibition.

**3. Many people regard pets as members of their family. What do you think?**
> It's largely accurate as a description of the relationship, and I'd resist being sniffy about it. People who describe a pet as family generally mean that the animal has a claim on their care and their finances. And that's exactly what family means in practice. Where it becomes unhelpful is when it obscures the animal's actual needs. So a dog gets treated as a small person and not as a dog. The sentiment is sound and the anthropomorphism that sometimes accompanies it isn't.

**4. Do many people keep pets in your country?**
> Far more than a generation ago, and the growth has been remarkably fast. Dogs and cats in the cities particularly, and the pet economy here is now enormous. Several things drove it: smaller households, more single people, an ageing population. And a generation with disposable income and no children yet. What's striking is the shift in status, since a dog in my grandmother's childhood was a working animal that lived outside. And my friends' dogs sleep on furniture and have insurance.

**5. What are the advantages of keeping a pet?**
> Companionship, most obviously, and for people living alone that's not a small thing. There's decent evidence on the health side too, lower stress markers and, for dog owners, considerably more walking. Which is probably doing most of the work. For children, there's the responsibility argument, though I'd note it only holds if the child is actually made responsible. And structure, which matters particularly for older or isolated people, since an animal requires you to get up and organise a day around something.

**6. Why do people always tell children stories with animals?**
> Partly a practical matter of attention, since children are drawn to animals before they're drawn to much else and a story with a wolf in it will be listened to. But the more interesting reason is that animals allow a difficult idea to be examined at a safe distance. A story about a rabbit who is frightened lets a child think about fear without it being about them. And that distance is what makes the lesson tolerable. It's also why the animals in those stories are never really animals.

> The film I'd describe is a comedy sequel that came out during Spring Festival a couple of years ago. I went with my family, because going to the cinema over that holiday is basically compulsory. I won't name it. Partly out of politeness, and partly because the specific title matters less than the pattern it belongs to. The original had been genuinely good. Everyone around me was crying, which did make me wonder whether I was being unreasonable. I've thought about that since. I don't think I was. Though I'd concede that objecting to a Spring Festival comedy for lacking integrity is a slightly ridiculous position to hold.

**Part3**

**1. What kinds of movies are the most popular in China?**
> Comedy dominates, particularly around Spring Festival, where family audiences are looking for something undemanding and the box office numbers are extraordinary. Beyond that, patriotic action films have been enormously successful over the last decade. And animated features drawing on Chinese mythology have had several major hits. What's shrunk is the middle-budget drama, which struggles to find an audience. And there's a durable appetite for anything with a strong emotional hook, since a film that makes people cry generates the word-of-mouth that drives ticket sales here.

**2. What are the differences between watching movies at home and in a cinema?**
> Attention, mostly, and it's a larger difference than the technical one. A cinema removes the option of stopping, checking your phone or talking. And a film that requires two uninterrupted hours works there and frequently fails at home. There's also the collective element, since comedy in particular is measurably funnier in a full room. What home offers is control and comfort, and for anything episodic that's clearly better. I'd say cinemas now suit a narrower band of films than they used to. And that band is mostly spectacle and comedy.

**3. Are movies more likely to help people become more creative than books?**
> Not more, and possibly less, though I'd resist ranking them. Reading requires you to construct the images yourself, which is genuine cognitive work. And a novel gives you access to interior states that film handles clumsily. Film's advantages are different: it's better at ambiguity of expression, at juxtaposition. And at conveying something in three seconds that would take a page. Where film loses out is pacing, since you're carried at the director's speed and can't pause to think without physically pausing.

**4. Can movies help people better understand the cultural background of a country?**
> To a point, and with a fairly large caveat about which country you're seeing. Films are extremely good at conveying texture, how people speak to each other, what a street sounds like, the rhythm of a meal. And those are the things travel writing conveys poorly. What they're bad at is representativeness, since films are made by a small and unusual subset of any society and are frequently designed for export. Somebody who understood China from its recent cinema would have a vivid and quite misleading picture.

**5. Should documentary films be completely truthful?**
> Truthful yes, comprehensive no, and the distinction matters. Every documentary selects, sequences and frames, and pretending otherwise is itself a form of dishonesty. So the claim to pure objectivity is the least trustworthy position available. What's legitimate is an argued perspective that doesn't falsify. What isn't is staging events presented as spontaneous, or editing an interview so that somebody appears to say something they didn't. The test I'd apply is whether the subject would recognise themselves in the result.

**6. Do people’s movie preferences change as they get older?**
> Substantially, and in a fairly consistent direction. People tend to move from plot towards character, so what seemed slow at twenty becomes the interesting part at forty. Tolerance for spectacle declines, and tolerance for ambiguity rises. There's also a shift in what people want films for, from stimulation towards recognition. And older viewers more often want to see something that reflects their own experience. My father watches almost nothing but documentaries now, which twenty years ago he'd have found unbearable.

> The ambition I'd describe is to become someone who designs systems, rather than only building the pieces of them. And to do that somewhere outside China for at least a few years. **Part3**

**1. Why should children have ambitions?**
> Because an ambition gives effort a direction, and children who have one tolerate difficulty that unfocused children don't. What I'd be careful about is the content of it. A specific ambition held too tightly at twelve is more likely to be a source of disappointment than of motivation, since almost nobody ends up doing what they named at that age. What's actually valuable is the disposition, the experience of wanting something enough to work at it. And that transfers even when the particular goal is abandoned.

**2. What do you think of people going after high positions?**
> It depends entirely on what they want the position for, and I'd resist judging it in general. Wanting authority because you have a view about how something should be done is a perfectly reasonable motivation. And organisations run badly when capable people are too fastidious to seek influence. What's less attractive is pursuing status as an end. Which tends to produce people who are good at being promoted and indifferent at the job. The difficulty is that the two are almost indistinguishable from outside.

**3. Why are some young people keen on being fans of superstars?**
> Partly it's ordinary identity-building, which every generation has done. And attaching yourself to somebody admired is a way of working out who you'd like to be. What's specific to now is the manufactured intimacy. The industry is deliberately constructed so that following somebody feels like a relationship, with constant access and apparent candour. Which is far more involving than the distant stardom of fifty years ago. Add a fan community that provides genuine belonging, and the appeal is fairly rational rather than foolish.

**4. Is it good for a person to be ambitious?**
> Generally yes, with a limit that's easy to miss. Ambition is what converts capability into anything. And people without it tend to be shaped by whatever was nearest rather than by what they chose. The failure mode is when the goal becomes the only measure of a life. So the present is permanently instrumental and nothing counts until the thing is achieved. Ambitious people are frequently miserable for precisely that reason. What seems to work is ambition about the work rather than about the position.

**5. Do you think it is necessary to be ambitious when working in a team in a company?**
> Not necessary, and in some forms actively harmful. Teams need a range, and a team composed entirely of ambitious people tends to compete internally over credit. Which is where a great deal of organisational dysfunction originates. What a team does need is people who care about the work being good. Which is distinct from caring about advancing. Some of the most valuable colleagues I've observed have no interest in promotion whatsoever and are the reason things actually function.

**6. Should parents support their children in pursuing their ambitions?**
> Support yes, direct no, and the distinction is where most of the difficulty lies. What support means concretely is providing information, access and a safety net. So the child can attempt something without the cost of failure being catastrophic. What it shouldn't mean is endorsing any ambition uncritically, since part of a parent's role is honest assessment. And a child pursuing something they have no aptitude for deserves to be told. The harder case is when the ambition is achievable and the parents simply disapprove of it.

# A law on environmental protection

**Part2** — Describe a law on environmental protection. You should say: What it is / How you first learned about it / Who benefits from it / And explain how you feel about this law

> The law I'd describe is the mandatory waste-sorting rule that's been rolled out across the big Chinese cities over the last few years. Shanghai was first, in 2019. Shenzhen brought in its own version not long after, so I've watched it arrive in my own building. What it requires is that household waste gets separated into categories. Roughly kitchen waste, recyclables, hazardous waste, and everything else. And it has to go to specific points, in some districts only during set hours. There are fines attached. And in the early stages there were volunteers standing at the bins, inspecting what people brought down, which caused a fair amount of complaint. How it's worked is more interesting than whether people liked it. Compliance was poor at first, and improved much faster than I expected. Largely because the enforcement was visible rather than severe. Nobody I know has actually been fined. What changed behaviour was somebody standing at the bin. My grandmother, incidentally, found the whole thing baffling. She'd been separating waste her entire life, and selling the recyclable part to a collector who came round the village. Why I'd choose this law is that it shows something about environmental policy in general. The measures that work are the ones that change the default, and make the wrong choice mildly inconvenient. Not the ones that appeal to your conscience.

**Part3**

**1. What kinds of rules do schools in China have?**
> A great many, and they extend well beyond academic conduct. There are rules about uniform and appearance, including hair length in some schools, about punctuality, about phone use. Which has tightened considerably recently, and in many schools about the physical arrangement of desks and belongings. There's also a structure around collective activity, morning exercises and flag ceremonies, that's close to universal. The general character is more prescriptive than in Western schools, with a stronger emphasis on uniformity. And how strictly any of it is enforced varies enormously.

**2. Do you think school rules are important?**
> Important in principle and frequently over-extended in practice. The defensible ones are those protecting the conditions for learning, on safety, on disruption, on attendance. And a classroom without those functions badly. Where it becomes harder to justify is the large body of rules about appearance and conformity. Which are usually defended on grounds of discipline and are really about control. What I'd apply is a simple test: whether the rule protects something or merely enforces uniformity. And a surprising number fail it.

**3. Are children unhappy with the school rules?**
> With some of them, certainly, and I'd say that's healthy rather than a problem. Children object most to rules whose purpose they can't see. Which tend to be the appearance rules rather than the safety ones. And they're generally quite accurate about which is which. What produces resentment isn't strictness, it's inconsistency, applying a rule to one student and not another, or enforcing something nobody can explain. Children have a fairly exact sense of fairness and notice arbitrariness immediately.

**4. How can parents and teachers help children understand and follow rules?**
> By explaining the reason, first, which is done far less often than it should be. A rule with a stated purpose can be evaluated and is much more likely to be internalised than one backed only by authority. Beyond that, by being consistent, since inconsistent enforcement teaches that rules are about power rather than principle. And by modelling it, because an adult who ignores rules that are inconvenient to them is teaching the actual lesson regardless of what they say.

**5. What are the rules people should obey at work?**
> The explicit ones are mostly about safety, confidentiality and conduct, and those are generally written down. The rules that matter more day to day are unwritten: responding within a reasonable time, not surprising colleagues in meetings, giving credit accurately. And being straightforward about problems early rather than concealing them. Those aren't in any handbook and they determine whether somebody is trusted. The transition from education to work is largely a matter of learning that second set.

**6. What is the purpose of punishment?**
> Several purposes that get conflated, which is why the debate is so unproductive. There's deterrence, which has reasonable evidence behind it, though the research consistently shows certainty matters more than severity. There's incapacitation, simply preventing further harm. There's rehabilitation, which is the stated aim of most modern systems and the least well funded. And there's retribution, which is the one people are least comfortable stating and which underlies a great deal of public sentiment. A system pursuing all four simultaneously tends to do none of them well.

**3. What skills can people learn from watching videos?**
> Anything demonstrable, essentially. Practical skills transfer extremely well, cooking, repairs, and a good deal of programming. And I picked up several of the tools I use daily at work from online tutorials rather than at university. Where video is weaker is anything requiring feedback, since you can watch correct technique indefinitely and still be doing it wrong without knowing. It's also poor for anything that needs sustained argument. So it's excellent for the visible layer of a skill and unreliable for the judgement underneath it.

**4. Are there any differences between the videos that young people and old people like to watch?**
> Substantial ones, though they've narrowed faster than people expect. Older viewers favour longer formats, television drama, historical and health content, and news. And they're considerably more likely to watch something through to the end. Younger viewers are on the short platforms and consume far more, far faster. What's converged is the platform itself, since older people in China adopted short video with remarkable speed. The content differs sharply, the medium much less.

**5. Are there any differences between the videos that young men and young women like to watch?**
> Some, though I'd be cautious, since the differences within each group are larger than those between them. The visible patterns are that gaming, sport and technology content skew male, while beauty, lifestyle and relationship content skew female, and those are strong enough to be commercially meaningful. What I'd question is how much is preference and how much is the recommendation algorithm. Which sorts people into categories early and then reinforces them. Two people with similar interests can end up with completely different feeds.

**6. What makes a video go viral online?**
> A strong emotional response in the first few seconds, essentially, and the specific emotion matters less than its intensity. Surprise, indignation and delight all work. Beyond that, shareability, which is subtly different from being good, since people share things that say something about them. Brevity and rewatchability help mechanically, because the platforms measure completion. And a substantial amount of luck, since near-identical videos perform completely differently depending on who happens to see them first.

# A new skill you learned when you were a child

**Part2** — Describe a new skill you learned when you were a child. You should say: What the skill was / Who taught you this skill / How you learned it / And explain how you felt about learning the skill

> The skill I'd describe is Chinese chess. I learnt it when I was about seven, from the grandfather of my friend Kai. That detail matters, because neither of my parents played. I'd probably never have picked it up otherwise. How I learnt it was the traditional way. Which is to say by losing continuously for about six months. He taught us on a folding table outside their building, on a paper board with plastic pieces. And he had one rule he never once relaxed. You don't take a move back. Ever. I found that unreasonable at seven. I now think it was the entire lesson. If you move without thinking, you live with it. And there's no faster way to learn to look before you commit. It took perhaps a year before I could give him a real game. I don't think I ever actually beat him. What I remember more clearly than the learning is a specific feeling. The point where the board stopped being a set of separate pieces and started being a shape. That happened quite suddenly. And I've had the same thing since with code, which I suspect is the same process. It's also the one skill from that period I've never lost. Kai and I still play, mostly online now. And he still wins.

**Part3**

**1. What skills should children learn before they go to school?**
> The genuinely foundational ones are social and physical rather than academic. Being able to separate from a parent without distress, to take turns, to express what they need in words. And to manage the basics of dressing and eating. Those determine whether a child can function in a classroom at all. On the academic side, I'd say being read to matters far more than being taught to read, since what a child needs at five is the expectation that books contain something worth having, not a decoding skill they'll acquire anyway.

**2. What are the differences between learning in a group and learning by yourself?**
> Group learning supplies motivation, correction and the considerable benefit of hearing other people's confusions, which frequently resolve your own. It also forces a pace, which people with weak self-discipline need. Learning alone gives you control over speed and direction. So you can spend three weeks on the part you find difficult rather than moving when the class moves. The serious weakness of learning alone is the absence of correction, since you can practise an error until it's permanent. The strongest arrangement is usually mostly alone with periodic expert checking.

**3. What are the differences between the way adults learn and the way children learn?**
> Children learn implicitly and adults learn deliberately, and each has a cost. A child absorbs structure without being able to state it. Which is why they acquire accents and adults don't, but it's slow and requires enormous exposure. Adults can use explicit strategy and prior knowledge to move much faster at the start. What adults lose is tolerance, since they've built an identity around competence and the beginner stage is genuinely unpleasant for them in a way it isn't for a child who expects to be bad at things.

**4. Do you think it is easier for children to learn new skills than for adults?**
> For some things decisively, for others not at all, and the popular version overstates it. Children have a real advantage in anything involving sound and motor patterns. So language and instruments and sport, where the developmental window is genuine. For anything requiring abstract reasoning or building on existing knowledge, adults learn considerably faster. The other half of the apparent advantage is circumstantial: children have time, instruction and permission to be incompetent. Which adults have to manufacture for themselves.

**5. Can a child learn skills from another child?**
> Certainly, and often more effectively than from an adult. A child who has just learnt something explains it in the language of somebody who remembers not understanding it. Which is exactly what an expert loses. There's also less status friction, so the learner will admit confusion they'd conceal from a teacher. Kai taught me more about chess in our first year than his grandfather did, simply by being two weeks ahead of me.

**6. Is it important for everyone to have a specialized skill?**
> Useful rather than necessary, and the case is weaker than it was. A specialised skill provides bargaining power, since something scarce commands a premium. And it gives people an identity to work from. What's changed is that specialisations now become obsolete within a career, so anyone who has only one is exposed. The more durable asset appears to be the capacity to acquire a new one, which is a different thing. Chen would say the specialism gets you in and the adaptability keeps you there.

# An important decision that you made

**Part2** — Describe an important decision that you made. You should say: What the decision was / How you made your decision / What the results of the decision were / And explain why it was important

> The decision I'd describe is turning down a job when I graduated. Which sounds straightforward, and was the hardest thing I've decided so far. I had two offers. One was from a large, well-known tech company. Paying around forty percent more. On a team maintaining an internal platform that was already built. The other was from a much smaller company, where the interview had basically been an argument about how you'd structure something. And where I'd be one of four people on the team rather than one of sixty. What made it genuinely difficult is that every external signal pointed the other way. My parents were clear about which one a sensible person takes. And they weren't being unreasonable. The name meant something, and the money was real. Several classmates thought I was posturing. How I decided, in the end, was by asking what I'd actually be doing on an ordinary Tuesday in each place. Rather than how each would look on a CV. At the bigger company, the honest answer was maintaining somebody else's architecture. Why it mattered is that almost everything since has followed from it. I've had my hands on decisions I wouldn't have touched for another five years elsewhere. And what I want now, to move towards system design and work abroad for a while, came directly out of finding that this is the part I care about. I earn less than most of my cohort. I'd make the same decision again without much hesitation.

**Part3**

**1. Do you think children sometimes have to make important decisions?**
> They do, and rather more than adults acknowledge. The decisions look small from outside. Which friendships to invest in, whether to admit a mistake, whether to keep going at something difficult, and those are formative. There are also genuinely consequential ones imposed early, since the Chinese system requires subject choices in mid-adolescence that constrain everything afterwards. What children lack isn't judgement so much as information and any sense of scale. Which is why the adult role is supplying context rather than deciding for them.

**2. What important decisions do teenagers need to make after graduation?**
> The obvious ones are what to study and where. Which in China are effectively determined by an examination result and are therefore less free than they appear. Beyond that, whether to leave home, which for a lot of people is the first real test of independence. And whether to prioritise a subject they like or one that's employable. That last one gets presented as a straightforward choice and rarely is. What I'd add is that the significant decisions at that age are often about who to spend time with. And nobody frames those as decisions at all.

**3. Who can children turn to for help when making a decision?**
> Parents first, though the usefulness depends heavily on whether the parents can distinguish advice from instruction. Teachers, particularly one who knows the child's work well. Beyond adults, older siblings and slightly older friends are consistently underrated, since they've just navigated the same decision and remember it accurately. When I was choosing between two job offers, the person who actually helped was someone two years ahead of me who'd made the opposite choice and was honest about regretting it.

**4. Do you think advertisements can influence our decisions when shopping?**
> Substantially, and increasingly at a level people can't observe in themselves. The straightforward effects are well known: familiarity makes a brand feel like a safer choice. And repetition does most of that work. What's changed is targeting, since an advertisement now arrives in response to something you did. Which makes it far harder to recognise as persuasion. I'd say the people most confident that advertising doesn't work on them are usually the clearest evidence that it does.

**5. Do you think the influence of advertising is good?**
> Mixed, and I'd separate the informational from the persuasive. Advertising that tells people something exists is genuinely useful, and it's how smaller businesses reach anyone at all. What's harder to defend is the machinery built around manufacturing dissatisfaction, since a business model that depends on people feeling insufficient has predictable social consequences. The children's dimension is the clearest case, and most countries restrict it for good reason. So the practice is legitimate and the current scale and targeting of it aren't obviously so.

**6. How do people usually make important decisions?**
> Badly, is the honest general answer, and mostly by reasoning backwards from a conclusion they arrived at quickly. The research on this is fairly consistent, that the decision is usually made intuitively and the deliberation supplies justification. What seems to improve matters is the unglamorous procedure: writing the alternatives down. Which prevents the quiet elimination of options, seeking out the strongest argument against what you want. And consulting somebody with no stake in it. And accepting that at some point the information runs out and you're choosing under uncertainty rather than calculating.

# A plan that you had to change recently

**Part2** — Describe a plan that you had to change recently. You should say: When this happened / What made you change the plan / What the new plan was / And how you felt about the change

> The plan I had to change was for this past summer. I'd arranged to go to Hangzhou for about ten days, with two friends. We'd booked the train. We had a rough plan built around the lake and the tea villages behind it. And I'd been looking forward to it since roughly February, mostly because I'd been once before and left feeling I'd rushed it. What happened was that the app I work on started crashing on a new iOS release, in late June. And it turned out to be structural rather than a simple bug. So the decision was taken to rework a substantial part of how we handle state. The timing overlapped almost exactly with the trip. Nobody ordered me to stay, I should say. Yaojin was explicit that the team could cover it. And I believe he meant it, which made the decision harder rather than easier. I changed the plan, obviously, or this wouldn't be much of a story. What I did was tell my friends about three weeks out. Which was late enough to be genuinely inconvenient, because they then had to decide whether to go without me. They did. Which was the right call, and mildly painful to see photographs of. How I feel about it is more mixed than a tidy answer would suggest. The rework was the best engineering I've done, and it shaped what I want to do next. But I've just described cancelling a holiday for a crash fix as a triumph. And I'm not entirely sure that's the lesson.

**Part3**

**1. Do people often change their plans?**
> Constantly, and rather more than they'd admit, since people remember the plans that survived. Most changes are minor rescheduling that nobody registers as a change at all. The substantial revisions tend to come from three sources: new information, a change in what the person wants, or somebody else's decision forcing it. What I'd observe is that people are considerably more willing to abandon plans they made alone than plans involving other people. Which is why group arrangements survive long after everyone has stopped wanting them.

**2. What kind of plans do people often make?**
> The bulk of it is short-horizon and logistical, meals, weekends, travel, which is planning in only a loose sense. The genuinely planned things tend to cluster around a few life decisions: education, career, whether and when to have children, buying property. And in China property planning starts remarkably early. What people plan least is the thing that most determines their satisfaction, which is who they spend time with. Almost nobody has a deliberate plan about friendship, and it's left entirely to circumstance.

**3. Would you tell others if you change your plan?**
> Yes, and as early as the change is certain, which is the part I got wrong. The obligation scales with how much the other person has committed. And my friends had booked transport, so three weeks' notice was late. What I've learnt is that the discomfort of telling somebody is almost always smaller than the cost of delay. And the delay is usually about managing your own feelings rather than protecting theirs. What matters is being straightforward about the reason rather than constructing a better-sounding one.

**4. Do you think people like the process of making plans more, or the moment of carrying them out?**
> Making them, for a great many people, and it's worth being honest about why. Planning is pleasurable because it's an unconstrained version of the thing, where nothing has gone wrong yet and the weather is good. Execution involves compromise and effort. There's a documented effect where anticipation produces more measured happiness than the event itself. I'd say the people who genuinely prefer the doing are those who've learnt to hold plans loosely. And they tend to be considerably better company on a holiday.

**5. Why do you think parents still make plans for their children nowadays?**
> Partly because the stakes genuinely are high here, since the education system is competitive and early decisions have long consequences, so parental involvement isn't irrational. Beyond that, there's a strong cultural expectation that a parent's responsibility extends well into adulthood, which isn't the case everywhere. And a more uncomfortable reason: for parents with one child, that child's trajectory carries the family's entire aspiration. Which produces a level of investment that's difficult to moderate.

**6. How does technology help people make plans?**
> Mostly by reducing the cost of coordination, which used to be the hard part. Arranging eight people for a meal once required a sequence of phone calls and now takes a group chat and a shared link. Beyond that, by supplying information that was previously guesswork, prices, schedules, how long something takes. Where it's less helpful is that it's made plans frictionless to change, so arrangements have become provisional in a way they weren't. And people now decide at the last moment because the technology permits it.

# An environmental law you would like your country to introduce

**Part2** — Describe an environmental law you would like your country to introduce. You should say: What law it should be / Why people should follow the law / Whether the law will be popular / And explain how you feel about this law

> The law I'd like introduced is a deposit return scheme for drink containers. So a small refundable charge on every bottle and can, which you get back when you return it. Several countries run versions of this. Germany and Norway are the best examples, and the return rates they get are above ninety percent. Which is far beyond anything ordinary recycling collection manages. Why this specifically? Because it works with the system China already has, rather than against it. There's an established informal recycling economy here. People who collect material and sell it on. My grandmother sold cardboard and bottles to a man with a tricycle throughout my childhood. That network is efficient. And the people in it are poorly paid and have no security. A deposit scheme would put a guaranteed floor price on exactly the material they already handle. How it would work is straightforward enough. The deposit gets added at purchase, and refunded at return points. In a Chinese city that could be built into the waste stations that already exist, without much difficulty. The crucial design point is that the deposit has to be big enough to be worth the inconvenience. The schemes that fail are the ones where the amount is trivial. What I find persuasive about it, compared with the waste-sorting rules we already have, is that it doesn't rely on conscience at all. It just makes throwing something away cost money. And the behaviour follows.

**Part3**

**1. How does technology affect the law?**
> Mostly by moving faster than it does, which is the structural problem rather than any particular failure. Legislation is reactive and deliberately slow, and technology now produces new categories of harm faster than any parliament can define them. So regulators are permanently addressing the previous problem. Beyond the pace issue, technology creates genuinely novel questions where existing concepts don't fit. And data, privacy and now generative systems are the obvious examples. It also changes enforcement, since surveillance capability has expanded enormously and the constraints on using it haven't kept up.

**2. What kinds of rules do schools in China have?**
> A great many, and they're more prescriptive than in most Western systems. There are academic and attendance rules, obviously, but also a substantial body governing appearance, uniform, hair length in many schools. And a structure of collective activity, morning exercises and flag ceremonies, that's close to universal. Phone restrictions have tightened considerably in recent years. The underlying emphasis is on uniformity and collective order. And how strictly any of it is enforced varies enormously between schools and between cities.

**3. Will there be a law that is universally accepted?**
> In a limited sense, and there are already some. The prohibitions on slavery, genocide and torture come close to universal formal acceptance, whatever the compliance record. Where I'd be sceptical is about anything more specific, since law encodes values and societies genuinely disagree about those. And a law accepted everywhere would have to be abstract enough to be nearly useless. The area where something like it may emerge is environmental, simply because the problem ignores borders and no state can solve it alone.

**4. What environmental laws does your country already have?**
> A fairly extensive framework, and considerably more than it's usually credited with. There's the Environmental Protection Law, substantially revised in 2015. Which introduced daily accumulating fines for continuing violations and gave NGOs standing to bring public interest cases. Beyond that, the mandatory waste-sorting regulations now in most major cities, an environmental protection tax, strict vehicle emissions standards. And the national carbon trading market launched in 2021. The persistent weakness has been enforcement at local level, where growth targets have historically competed with compliance.
